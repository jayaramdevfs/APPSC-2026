## Unit 1: Discovery and Excavation

The Harappan civilization was not known to the modern world until relatively recently. The site of Harappa in Punjab was noticed by the British general Cunningham as early as 1853, but it was not properly excavated at that time. The real discovery came in 1921, when Daya Ram Sahni began systematic excavations at Harappa. Almost simultaneously, R.D. Banerjee excavated Mohenjo-daro in Sindh. John Marshall, then Director General of the Archaeological Survey of India, brought the findings to international attention in 1931 with his landmark publication on the Indus Valley Civilization. Further major work was done by Ernest Mackay at Mohenjo-daro in 1938, and Mortimer Wheeler brought new rigour to the excavations in 1946, introducing the concept of stratigraphic excavation to Indian archaeology.

The civilization is referred to by several names. "Harappan" is the most precise term used by archaeologists, since Harappa was the first site to be excavated. "Indus Valley Civilization" is the popular name, though somewhat misleading because the civilization extended far beyond the Indus valley. "Indus Civilization" is also commonly used. R.S. Sharma uses "Harappan culture" throughout, as it is archaeologically the most appropriate.

## Unit 2: Geographic Extent

The Harappan civilization was one of the largest civilizations of the ancient world. It covered approximately 1,299,600 square kilometres — an area larger than Pakistan, larger than Egypt, and larger than Mesopotamia. Its reach extended from the foothills of the Siwaliks in the north all the way to the Arabian Sea coast in the south, and from the Makran coast (in present-day Balochistan) in the west to as far east as Meerut in Uttar Pradesh.

In total, about 2,800 Harappan sites have been identified. Of these, 1,022 belong to the mature phase of the civilization — 406 in Pakistan and 616 in India. The sheer number of sites in India means that the civilization was not confined to Pakistan (as people sometimes assume), but was in fact predominantly spread across the Indian subcontinent.

The most important cities were:

**Harappa** in Punjab (Pakistan), on the left bank of the Ravi river — the first excavated site and the one that gave the civilization its name. Harappa was a major urban centre with massive granaries and a large working-class residential area.

**Mohenjo-daro** in Sindh (Pakistan), on the right bank of the Indus — the largest city of the civilization, covering about 500 hectares. Its name in Sindhi means "mound of the dead." It had the most impressive urban planning features: the Great Bath, the largest granary, and well-organized residential blocks.

**Chanhu-daro** in Sindh — a smaller site notable for evidence of craft production, particularly bead-making and seal-making.

**Lothal** in Gujarat — an important port city. Its significance lies in two things: evidence of rice cultivation (earliest in the subcontinent, from around 1800 BCE), and a dockyard structure that is considered the earliest example of a tidal dock in the world.

**Kalibangan** in Rajasthan (on the banks of the dried Ghaggar river, which is identified with the ancient Sarasvati river) — whose name means "black bangles" in Hindi, referring to the bangles found there. Kalibangan shows evidence of a pre-Harappan ploughed field — the earliest evidence of ploughing anywhere in the subcontinent.

**Banawali** in Haryana — another mature-phase city on the Ghaggar-Hakra plain.

**Dholavira** in Gujarat (Rann of Kutch) — a later excavation of great importance. At 50 hectares within the walled area, it is notably different from other Harappan cities because its citadel was made of stone rather than baked brick — the only such exception found so far.

**Rakhigarhi** in Haryana — the largest Harappan site within present-day India, covering approximately 250 hectares. It has attracted attention in recent decades for its DNA studies.

**Sutkagendor** on the Makran coast (Balochistan) — one of the westernmost Harappan sites, indicating the civilization's reach along the Arabian Sea coast, possibly a trading post.

**Surkotada** in Gujarat — notable for claimed evidence of horse remains, though the dating and identification of these remains is disputed.

## Unit 3: Town Planning and Architecture

The most striking feature of Harappan civilization is its extraordinary town planning. Looking at the layout of cities like Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, we see a degree of urban organization that was not seen again in India for thousands of years.

Every major Harappan city was divided into two distinct parts: the **citadel** (a raised mound to the west, enclosed by a wall) and the **lower town** (a larger residential and commercial area to the east). The citadel contained public buildings — the great bath, granary, assembly halls — while the lower town contained residential quarters for ordinary citizens.

The streets were laid out on a strict grid pattern. The main streets ran north-south or east-west, and smaller lanes connected to them. Where lanes met main streets, the corners of houses were rounded or cut at an angle — this shows that the city planners were thinking about cart traffic turning corners. The grid planning indicates centralized authority capable of enforcing a uniform design across an entire city.

The **Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro** is one of the most impressive structures. It measures 11.88 metres long, 7.01 metres wide, and 2.43 metres deep. The floor and walls were made of closely fitted baked bricks with layers of bitumen to prevent water seepage. Steps led down from both ends. It was surrounded by corridors, galleries, and rooms. The purpose was almost certainly ritual — public bathing for religious purification, similar to the concept of the sacred tank found later in Hindu temples. It is the earliest known public water tank in the world.

The **granaries** were another monumental achievement. At Mohenjo-daro, the granary measured 45.71 metres by 15.23 metres — a massive structure for storing grain. At Harappa, there were six granaries arranged symmetrically, each 15.23 metres by 6.09 metres, positioned near the river to facilitate loading and unloading. The presence of granaries near the citadel, under what was presumably the control of the ruling authority, tells us that grain was a primary form of wealth and probably also used as a form of taxation.

Houses were built of **burnt bricks** — this is crucial. Egypt and Mesopotamia, the other great Bronze Age civilizations, used sun-dried mud bricks. Only the Harappans used kiln-fired bricks, which are much stronger and more durable. The bricks were of standardized sizes, with a consistent ratio of length:width:thickness (approximately 4:2:1), uniform across the entire civilization. This standardization over such a large geographic area tells us that either there was a strong central authority, or that there was deep cultural uniformity across all Harappan settlements.

Houses typically had a central courtyard with rooms opening onto it. Most houses had their own bathroom and latrine. The waste water drained into small drains that connected to larger covered street drains. The **drainage system** of the Harappan cities was the most sophisticated of the ancient world. Every street had an underground drain — covered with bricks that could be removed for cleaning. Soak pits were built at intervals. R.S. Sharma notes: "Perhaps no other Bronze Age civilization paid so much attention to health and cleanliness." This concern for sanitation suggests that they understood the connection between dirty water and disease, even without our modern germ theory.

## Unit 4: Agriculture and Food Production

The Harappan economy was fundamentally agrarian. The civilization flourished in the flood plains of major rivers — the Indus, the Ghaggar-Hakra (ancient Sarasvati), and their tributaries — where annual floods deposited fresh silt and maintained soil fertility. Unlike Mesopotamia, where elaborate canal irrigation was necessary, Harappan agriculture appears to have relied mainly on flood-recession farming: planting in the soil moistened by receding floods.

The main food crops were **wheat and barley**, which were the staples. Other crops included rai (mustard greens), peas, sesamum, and mustard. Cotton was cultivated in the Sindh region — the Harappans were the first people in the world to grow and spin cotton into cloth. When the Greeks later encountered cotton through trade, they named it after Sindh, calling the region "Sindon" (from which we get the word "sindoor" or "sindon" in medieval Latin). Rice was also cultivated, though later — the earliest evidence comes from Lothal in Gujarat, from around 1800 BCE.

The plough was used in Harappan agriculture. At Kalibangan, archaeologists found a ploughed field with a criss-cross pattern preserved in the soil — the furrows ran in two directions at right angles to each other, a practice used today to plant two different crops in the same field. This is the earliest direct evidence of ploughing anywhere in the Indian subcontinent.

Animals domesticated by the Harappans included oxen (used to pull ploughs and carts), buffaloes, goats, sheep, and pigs. The **humped bull** (zebu) held a special place — it appears on the largest number of Harappan seals, suggesting cultural or religious significance. Elephants and rhinoceroses were also known to them, based on depictions on seals, though these may have been wild animals.

The question of the **horse** is archaeologically significant. R.S. Sharma carefully notes that while bones claimed to be horse have been found at Surkotada (Gujarat, dating to around 2000 BCE) and in superficial layers at Mohenjo-daro, these identifications are doubted by specialists. The Harappan civilization was decidedly NOT centred around the horse — there are no unambiguous horse bones, no horse-drawn chariots, and no clear depictions of horses in Harappan art. This contrasts sharply with the Vedic culture, which was deeply horse-centred (the horse sacrifice, the horse-drawn chariot, the ubiquity of horses in the Rigveda). This contrast becomes important when we discuss whether Harappans could have been the same people as the Rig Vedic Aryans.

## Unit 5: Technology, Crafts, and Industry

The Harappans worked with **bronze** — an alloy of copper and tin. Copper came from the Khetri mines in Rajasthan, while tin had to be imported from Afghanistan. The working of bronze required knowledge of metallurgy, long-distance trade networks, and skilled craftsmen. Bronze tools and weapons found include axes, chisels, knives, and spearheads.

The famous **bronze dancing girl** from Mohenjo-daro is one of the most iconic objects of ancient India. It is a small figurine (about 11 cm tall) of a naked young woman standing in a casual contrapposto pose, wearing bangles on one arm and a necklace. She is quite unlike the stiff formal poses of Egyptian or Mesopotamian art — she conveys natural movement and confidence. The term "dancing girl" is our modern label; we do not know her actual identity.

A **steatite statue of a male figure** wearing an ornamented robe (with trefoil patterns filled with red pigment) was found at Mohenjo-daro. Some scholars identify this as a priest-king figure, though R.S. Sharma is cautious about this interpretation.

Cotton textile production was significant. **Spindle whorls** (small disk-shaped weights used to spin thread) are found in large numbers across Harappan sites, indicating that cotton spinning and weaving was a widespread domestic activity. The Harappans may have been the first civilization to weave cotton into cloth.

**Pottery** was made on the wheel and fired in kilns. The most characteristic Harappan pottery is red or pink with black paintings — geometric designs, animals, and tree motifs. The quality is remarkably uniform across the vast geographic range of the civilization, another indicator of deep cultural coherence.

**Bead making** was an important industry. Beads were made from carnelian (a red semi-precious stone from Gujarat), lapis lazuli (from Afghanistan), gold, silver, faience (glazed clay), and shell. The craftsmanship was extraordinarily fine — some carnelian beads are drilled with holes barely visible to the naked eye, requiring tools we still cannot fully replicate. These beads were trade items exchanged across vast distances.

**Seals** were made primarily from steatite (soapstone). About 2,000 seals have been found at Harappan sites. They typically show an animal (most commonly the unicorn — a bull-like animal with a single horn; also buffalo, tiger, rhinoceros, goat, elephant, crocodile) above a manger-like object, with a line of script at the top. Seals served as markers of identity, authority, and ownership — probably used to seal containers of goods for trade. Some scholars believe they may have been used as amulets.

**Boat building** is indicated by depictions on seals and by the existence of the Lothal dockyard, suggesting maritime capability for coastal trade.

**Goldsmith work** is evident from ornaments found at various sites — gold necklaces, earrings, bangles, and hair ornaments have been recovered.

## Unit 6: Trade and Commerce

Trade was the engine that drove Harappan prosperity. The civilization was remarkably well connected — both internally and with distant lands.

Internal trade was enabled by several key innovations. **Standardized weights** were used across the entire civilization. These weights follow a binary system, doubling from 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, and then moving to 160, 320, 640 — the multiples of 16 are significant. R.S. Sharma notes that this tradition of 16 persisted: right into modern times, 16 annas made one rupee. Uniform weights across 1.3 million square kilometres implies not just trade but a sophisticated commercial culture.

The **script** on seals (discussed in the next unit) functioned as labels for goods, names of merchants, or symbols of authority — enabling commercial transactions across communities that may not have shared a spoken language.

The **uniform brick sizes**, uniform pottery styles, and uniform weights and measures all indicate an integrated economic zone, not a loose collection of independent city-states.

**External trade** was conducted with Mesopotamia, and the evidence is rich and detailed. Mesopotamian records from the 3rd millennium BCE refer to a place called "Meluha" from which they imported carnelian beads, lapis lazuli, copper, ivory, pearls, and fine textiles. Scholars are confident that Meluha = the Harappan region. Dilmun (identified as modern Bahrain) served as an intermediary trading post in the Arabian Gulf.

Harappan merchants sailed along the Makran coast and through the Arabian Sea. The Lothal dockyard — a large rectangular basin connected to the Sabarmati river estuary — was designed so that ships could enter at high tide and be loaded or unloaded as the tide receded. This is the earliest known purpose-built dock in the world.

Lapis lazuli, found in Harappan jewellery, could only have come from mines in northeastern Afghanistan (Badakhshan). The trade route to bring this stone to the Indus valley was thousands of kilometres long and passed through difficult terrain. The existence of this trade shows the reach of Harappan commercial networks.

The trading system was based on **barter**, not coinage — no Harappan coins have been found. But the standardized weights ensured that barter transactions were fair and consistent.

## Unit 7: Social Organization and Polity

Who governed the Harappan cities? This is one of the great unsolved questions of ancient Indian history. Because the Harappan script has not been deciphered, we have no written records of kings, laws, or administrative structures.

R.S. Sharma analyses this through inference from archaeology. He observes that in Egypt and Mesopotamia, temples and palaces are the most prominent buildings — the religious authority (priest) and political authority (king) are clearly visible in the archaeological record. In Harappan sites, neither grand temples nor royal palaces have been identified. The Great Bath is a public building, possibly for ritual use, but it is not a temple dedicated to a deity in the way Egyptian temples were. The granaries suggest administrative control of grain surplus, but no "palace" complex has been found where a king would have lived.

From this, R.S. Sharma suggests that Harappan society may have been governed by a **merchant oligarchy** — a class of wealthy traders who controlled the citadel, the granary, and trade. The citadel represented sovereign power; the granary was the treasury; taxes may have been collected in kind (grain). This would explain the civilization's obsession with commercial infrastructure: standardized weights, seals, trade networks — the priorities of a merchant class rather than a warrior king.

The enormous uniformity of culture across the vast Harappan region — uniform brick sizes, pottery, weights, urban planning — suggests some form of overarching authority that maintained standards. Whether this was a single political empire or a network of culturally unified city-states remains debated.

**Social hierarchy** certainly existed. At Harappa, a workers' residential area with rows of small single-room barracks-like structures was found near the granaries. This suggests that some people did heavy labour (perhaps grain processing) in organized, controlled conditions — possibly a form of bonded labour or state corvée work. Meanwhile, larger houses in the lower town clearly belonged to people of higher status. The civilization was not egalitarian.

## Unit 8: Religion and Beliefs

The religious life of the Harappans must be pieced together from material evidence alone — seals, figurines, and structures — since the script is undeciphered.

**The Mother Goddess** was a central figure. Thousands of terracotta figurines of a female figure have been found across Harappan sites. The most significant type shows a female figure with elaborate headdress, sometimes with a plant growing from her embryo — identifying her as a goddess of earth and fertility. This "earth mother" concept — the goddess as the source of all vegetation and life — is ancient and widespread. She persisted in later Indian religion as Shakti, Durga, and other forms of the feminine divine.

**A Male Deity** appears on a famous seal from Mohenjo-daro, sometimes called the "Pashupati seal." It shows a seated figure in a yogic posture, with three faces (or one face with horns making it appear to have three), wearing a horned headdress. He is surrounded by four animals: elephant, tiger, rhinoceros, and buffalo; and below his throne are two deer. Some scholars identify this as a proto-Shiva figure — "Pashupati" means "lord of animals," and Shiva is later associated with yoga, animals, and the bull. R.S. Sharma notes this interpretation cautiously — the connection is plausible but not proven.

**Tree worship** was clearly important. The pipal tree (sacred fig, Ficus religiosa) appears frequently on seals and painted pottery. In later Hinduism and Buddhism, the pipal has sacred significance (it is the Bodhi tree under which the Buddha attained enlightenment). The Harappan evidence suggests this reverence for the pipal goes back at least 4,500 years.

**Animal worship** is evident from the prominence of animals on seals. The unicorn (a bull-like animal with a single curved horn) is the most frequently depicted — no one knows what this animal represents. The humped bull (zebu) is the second most common. Whether these animals were worshipped or simply used as clan or merchant symbols is unclear.

**Phallus (linga) worship** is indicated by polished stone conical objects that bear a strong resemblance to the linga, the symbol of Shiva in later Hindu worship. If these are indeed lingas, it suggests the Shaiva tradition of worship has very ancient roots.

**Amulets** were widely used — small objects worn on the body to ward off evil forces, disease, and bad luck. This suggests belief in supernatural forces that could harm humans and that could be countered through ritual objects.

## Unit 9: Script, Weights, and Measures

The **Harappan script** remains one of the great mysteries of ancient history. About 4,000 inscribed objects have been found — mostly short inscriptions on seals, with the average inscription being only 5 signs long. The total number of distinct signs is estimated at between 250 and 400.

The script runs from right to left (in at least some inscriptions). It is not alphabetical (where each sign represents a sound). Most scholars believe it is a logo-syllabic script — some signs represent whole words, others represent syllables. Despite decades of effort, the script has not been convincingly deciphered. Because there is no bilingual inscription (like the Rosetta Stone for Egyptian hieroglyphics), we cannot use a known language to crack the code.

R.S. Sharma notes that the script is clearly indigenous — it was not derived from Mesopotamian or Egyptian writing systems. The Harappans developed their own writing independently.

The **standardized weights** deserve special attention. The weights follow a precise mathematical sequence based on multiples of 16. The smallest weights are tiny cubes of chert (a type of flint). The larger weights are of alabaster, limestone, or other stones. These weights were used across the entire civilization — archaeologists find the same weight standards from sites in Balochistan to Gujarat. This is remarkable: you could take a weight from Mohenjo-daro and use it accurately in a market in Lothal, over 1,000 km away.

The emphasis on 16 as the base unit is historically persistent. The Indian monetary system right up to 1957 (when India decimalized its currency) used 16 annas to the rupee. The traditional system of 16 chhatanks to the ser, 16 seers to a maund — these may be traced in concept, if not in direct continuity, to this 4,500-year-old tradition.

## Unit 10: Decline of the Civilization

The Harappan civilization reached its mature phase between approximately 2500 BCE and 1900 BCE. After this, it entered a phase of decline. The great cities were gradually abandoned, and by around 1700 BCE, urban life in the Indus valley had effectively ended. What caused this collapse?

R.S. Sharma discusses multiple factors:

**Environmental change** was probably the most fundamental cause. The Ghaggar-Hakra river system (which many scholars identify with the ancient Sarasvati river) was a major lifeline for hundreds of Harappan settlements in Rajasthan and Haryana. Around 1700 BCE, tectonic shifts caused the Yamuna and Sutlej rivers to change course and stop feeding into the Ghaggar-Hakra system. The river dried up. Hundreds of settlements lost their water supply. This forced large-scale migration eastward and southward.

**Trade collapse** was another factor. The civilization's prosperity depended significantly on trade with Mesopotamia. Around 2000 BCE, the rise of the Elamite kingdom in Iran disrupted the trade routes. Mesopotamian records stop mentioning Meluha after about 1900 BCE — the trade connection was broken. Without the economic engine of long-distance trade, the urban centres lost their reason to exist.

**Soil exhaustion** may have contributed in some areas. Intensive farming on the same land over centuries, combined with deforestation to fuel the brick kilns, could have degraded the agricultural base that sustained the cities.

R.S. Sharma explicitly does NOT endorse the old theory that the Harappan civilization was destroyed by an Aryan invasion. The archaeological evidence for a violent, catastrophic end is not convincing at most sites. Some skeletons in upper layers at Mohenjo-daro were once cited as evidence of massacre, but these are now understood as a very late deposit, not evidence of a sudden violent conquest of the entire civilization.

## Unit 11: The Post-Urban Phase (1900–1200 BCE)

The end of the mature Harappan phase did not mean the end of Harappan culture. A post-urban phase followed, lasting roughly from 1900 to 1200 BCE. During this period, people carrying Harappan cultural traditions — pottery styles, some craft techniques, religious practices — spread across a wide area: Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Gujarat, Rajasthan, and as far as Jammu.

These post-urban communities were smaller, less organized, and less wealthy than the mature phase cities. But they represent continuity, not complete rupture. Many elements of Harappan material culture survived and were absorbed into later Indian cultures.

In Gujarat, late Harappan settlements merged with the Cemetery H culture (found at Harappa) and the Jhukar culture (in Sindh). These regional cultures show a mixture of Harappan traditions with new elements that may indicate contact with incoming populations from Central Asia.

## Unit 12: Origins — The Harappan–Vedic Question

A question of enormous importance for Indian history is: what is the relationship between the Harappan civilization and the Vedic civilization? Were the Harappans the same people as the Rig Vedic Aryans?

R.S. Sharma argues clearly that they were NOT the same.

The evidence:
1. **The horse problem**: The Rigveda is saturated with references to horses — horse sacrifice (Ashvamedha), horse-drawn chariots, the god Indra fighting from a chariot. The horse was central to Vedic culture. The Harappan civilization shows no convincing evidence of the domesticated horse — no unambiguous horse bones, no chariots, no horse-centred ritual. A civilization that used the horse as extensively as the Rigveda describes would leave abundant horse remains.

2. **Dating**: The mature Harappan phase ended around 1900 BCE. The Rigveda is dated by most scholars to around 1500–1200 BCE. The Harappan cities were long abandoned by the time the Rigveda was being composed.

3. **Pre-Harappan settlements in Balochistan**: Settlements going back to 7000 BCE have been found in Balochistan — at Mehrgarh and other sites. By 4000–3000 BCE, settled farming communities were already evolving complex material cultures. The site of Rahman Dheri (in Pakistan's North-West Frontier Province) appears to be the earliest planned town in the subcontinent, predating the mature Harappan phase. This suggests the Harappan civilization developed indigenously from these pre-Harappan Baluchistan cultures, NOT from an external Aryan migration.

The Harappan civilization appears to have been a Bronze Age urban civilization of indigenous origin, which developed from the farming cultures of greater Balochistan and eventually collapsed due to environmental and economic factors. The Vedic culture that we see in the Rigveda was a different, later cultural tradition — partly introduced by Indo-European-speaking pastoralists from Central Asia and partly synthesized with surviving indigenous traditions.

## Summary — The Big Picture

The Harappan civilization stands as one of the great achievements of ancient humanity:

- **Largest Bronze Age civilization by area** (1.3 million sq km — greater than Egypt or Mesopotamia)
- **Earliest urban sanitation system** in the world
- **First cotton growers** and weavers in the world
- **Most sophisticated Bronze Age drainage** system
- **Standardized weights and measures** across 1.3 million sq km
- **Earliest tidal dock** (Lothal)
- **Script** — still undeciphered
- **Religion** — elements that feed into later Hinduism (Mother Goddess, proto-Shiva, pipal worship, linga)

Yet it collapsed, leaving no deciphered texts, no named kings, no founding myths, and no direct successor civilization. It was so thoroughly forgotten that its very existence was unknown until 1921. The Harappans were urban when northern Europe was in the Stone Age — and then they simply vanished from history for 4,000 years.
