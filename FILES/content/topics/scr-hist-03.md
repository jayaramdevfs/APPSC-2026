# Modern India: From European Arrival to Independence
> *Modern India's story is fundamentally one of resistance — how a civilization that was colonized and drained rebuilt itself, redefined its identity, and eventually forced the most powerful empire in history to leave.*

## The Big Picture

The British East India Company arrived in India as traders in 1600. Within 250 years, they controlled the entire subcontinent. How this happened — and how it was reversed — is the central drama of modern Indian history. The British succeeded not through superior civilization but through superior organization: divide-and-rule politics, military technology, and the systematic extraction of India's wealth to fund British industrialization.

The nationalist response was equally organized. Three generations of Indians — moderate lawyers petitioning in English, extremist activists burning foreign cloth, and Gandhian masses refusing to cooperate — each contributed a layer to the freedom movement. Understanding the **evolution of nationalism** from moderate petitions (1885) to Quit India (1942) is as important as memorizing individual events.

## 1. The Advent of Europeans (15th–18th Century)

### Arrival Order and Key Facts

The **Portuguese** came first. Vasco da Gama's arrival at Calicut in **1498** opened the sea route from Europe to India, breaking the Arab monopoly on Indian Ocean trade. The Portuguese established their capital at Goa (1530) and introduced the printing press and tobacco to India. Crucially, they were also the **last to leave** — Goa was only liberated in 1961.

The **Dutch** (VOC, 1605) established their first factory at **Masulipatnam** (Andhra) — a fact that directly connects to AP exam questions. They focused primarily on the Indonesian spice trade and were effectively eliminated by the British at the **Battle of Bedara (1759)**.

The **English East India Company** (chartered 1600) established its first factory at **Surat (1613)** and built its three great fortresses: **Fort St. George** (Madras, 1639), **Fort William** (Calcutta), and Bombay — acquired from Portugal as part of a royal marriage dowry in 1661.

The **French** (1664) were Britain's most serious European rival. Their East India Company was state-sponsored by Colbert, with headquarters at **Pondicherry**. The Anglo-French rivalry played out through three **Carnatic Wars (1746–1763)**, ending with the French confined to Pondicherry after the **Battle of Wandiwash (1760)**.

| Power | Arrived | First Indian Base | Eliminated By |
|-------|---------|-------------------|--------------|
| Portuguese | 1498 | Calicut → Goa | Stayed till 1961 |
| Dutch | 1605 | Masulipatnam (AP) | British (Bedara 1759) |
| English | 1600 | Surat (1613) | — (victors) |
| Danes | 1616 | Tranquebar, Serampore | Sold to British (1845) |
| French | 1664 | Surat → Pondicherry | Confined after Wandiwash (1760) |

### British Conquest: The Key Battles

The British conquered India in three decisive moves. **Bengal** was taken first: the **Battle of Plassey (1757)** — where Robert Clive defeated Nawab Siraj-ud-Daula through treachery (Mir Jafar betrayed the Nawab) — gave the Company enormous financial resources. The **Battle of Buxar (1764)** was the real test: Hector Munro defeated the combined forces of Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daula, and Shah Alam II, and the **Treaty of Allahabad** gave the Company the **Diwani** (revenue rights) of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa.

**Mysore** resisted hardest. Haidar Ali and his son **Tipu Sultan** fought four wars (1767–1799) and came close to driving the British out of South India. Tipu's death defending **Srirangapatna** in 1799 ended independent Mysore. The **Marathas** collapsed through internal division in three wars (1775–1818). Finally, Punjab fell after two **Sikh Wars (1845–1849)** following Ranjit Singh's death.

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** Britain's financial lifeline from India's conquest: Plassey gave them Bengal's treasury; Buxar gave them Bengal's revenue. Without this wealth, Britain could not have funded the Industrial Revolution at the scale it did.

## 2. Colonial Administration and Economic Impact

### The Land Revenue Systems

The British needed revenue above all else, and they extracted it through three different land settlement systems applied to different regions.

The **Permanent Settlement** (Cornwallis, 1793) fixed Bengal's land revenue permanently with zamindars, making them legally into landlords. This created a loyal class of absentee landlords who collected rent from peasants but had no incentive to invest in agriculture. The **Ryotwari System** (Thomas Munro, 1820) in Madras and Bombay dealt directly with individual cultivators — but imposed brutal tax rates (often 50%+) that drove farmers into debt. The **Mahalwari System** (Holt Mackenzie, 1822) in North India settled with village communities as a whole.

| System | Who Introduced | Where | Key Effect |
|--------|---------------|-------|-----------|
| Permanent Settlement | Cornwallis (1793) | Bengal, Bihar, Orissa | Created landlord class; peasant exploitation |
| Ryotwari | Thomas Munro (1820) | Madras, Bombay, Assam | Direct peasant taxation; rural debt |
| Mahalwari | Holt Mackenzie (1822) | NWFP, Central India, Punjab | Village community settlement |

### The Drain of Wealth

**Dadabhai Naoroji** in his landmark work *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* quantified what he called the **Drain of Wealth** — the systematic transfer of India's resources to Britain through "Home Charges" (payments for India's administration, wars, and debt serviced in London), discriminatory tariffs that destroyed Indian industries, and the forced commercialization of agriculture.

> 📌 **Exam Note:** The **Drain Theory** was advanced by Dadabhai Naoroji (Poverty and Un-British Rule), R.C. Dutt (Economic History of India), and later developed by Romesh Chunder Dutt. Naoroji estimated India's annual drain at £12–30 million.

The destruction of Indian handicrafts — particularly the handloom textile industry — through cheap British machine-made cloth was catastrophic. Where Bengal had once exported fine muslin, it now imported British cotton. India was transformed from a manufacturing exporter to a raw material supplier.

## 3. The Revolt of 1857

### The Accumulating Powder Keg

The Revolt of 1857 was the most serious challenge to British rule in the 19th century. It was triggered by a cartridge but fueled by decades of accumulated grievances. **Lord Dalhousie's Doctrine of Lapse** had annexed Satara (1848), Jhansi (1853), and Nagpur (1853) by refusing to recognize adopted heirs. The **annexation of Awadh (1856)** — on grounds of "misgovernance" — alienated both its aristocracy and its large population of sepoys from Awadh who formed the backbone of the Bengal Army.

The immediate trigger was the **Enfield rifle cartridge**, greased (reportedly) with beef and pork fat — offensive to both Hindu and Muslim soldiers who had to bite the cartridge before loading.

| Center | Military/Political Leaders |
|--------|--------------------------|
| Delhi | Bahadur Shah Zafar (symbolic), General Bakht Khan (real) |
| Kanpur | Nana Sahib, Tantia Tope |
| Lucknow | Begum Hazrat Mahal |
| Jhansi | Rani Laxmibai |
| Bihar (Arrah) | Kunwar Singh (80-year-old zamindar) |

> ⚠️ **Exam Trap:** The Revolt of 1857 is called India's "First War of Independence" by V.D. Savarkar, but **British historians** called it a "Sepoy Mutiny." The APPSC syllabus treats it as a revolt with both military and civilian dimensions.

The revolt's suppression led to the **Government of India Act (1858)**: EIC rule abolished, India transferred to the British Crown, the Governor-General redesignated as **Viceroy** (Lord Canning was the first).

## 4. Socio-Religious Reform Movements (19th–20th Century)

### Reform From Within

The 19th century saw Indian intellectuals respond to colonialism by reforming their own society. **Raja Ram Mohan Roy** (Brahmo Samaj, 1828) was the pivotal figure — the "Father of Modern India" — who campaigned against Sati, advocated women's education, and used both Western rationalism and Hindu scriptures to argue for reform. Lord Bentinck abolished Sati in **1829** responding to Roy's campaign.

**Swami Dayananda Saraswati** (Arya Samaj, 1875) took a different approach — "Go back to Vedas" — rejecting idol worship, caste discrimination, and untouchability while championing the Shuddhi movement for reconversion. **Swami Vivekananda** (Ramakrishna Mission, 1897) presented Hinduism as a rational, universal religion at the 1893 Chicago Parliament of Religions.

The reform impulse crossed religious communities. **Sir Syed Ahmad Khan** promoted Western education among Muslims through the MAO College (later AMU, Aligarh). **Jyotiba Phule** (Satyashodhak Samaj, 1873) fought Brahmin dominance in Maharashtra. **Periyar** (E.V. Ramasamy Naicker) launched the Self-Respect Movement in Tamil Nadu.

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** The reform movements were a necessary precondition for nationalism. They created an educated class that could conceive of a unified "India" and argue for rights in the colonizer's language.

## 5. The National Movement: Three Generations

### The Moderates (1885–1905): Pray, Petition, Protest

The **Indian National Congress** was founded in 1885 in Bombay by A.O. Hume (a retired British civil servant). **W.C. Bonnerjee** was the first president; the first session had 72 delegates. The Moderate leaders — **Dadabhai Naoroji**, **Gopal Krishna Gokhale**, **Pherozeshah Mehta** — believed in constitutional methods, working within the British system. Their contribution was laying the organizational foundation and articulating India's economic grievances in terms the British parliament could understand.

> ⚠️ **Exam Trap:** The INC was founded in 1885 under Viceroy **Lord Dufferin** — NOT Lord Curzon (who was Viceroy 1899–1905).

### The Extremists (1905–1919): Swaraj as Birthright

**Lord Curzon's partition of Bengal (1905)** — ostensibly for administrative convenience but actually to divide Hindu and Muslim communities — ignited the **Swadeshi and Boycott Movement**. Burning foreign cloth, promoting indigenous industries, and chanting *Vande Mataram* — this was a new kind of politics. **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** declared: "Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it." The trio of **Lal-Bal-Pal** (Lala Lajpat Rai, Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal) pushed Congress toward demanding self-rule. At the **Surat Split (1907)**, Congress formally divided into Moderates and Extremists.

### The Gandhian Era (1915–1947): Mass Civil Disobedience

Gandhi returned from South Africa in 1915 and transformed Indian nationalism from an elite movement into a mass one. His genius was finding everyday acts — making salt, spinning khadi, refusing to board a train carriage — that turned ordinary Indians into political actors.

His early campaigns tested Satyagraha in Indian conditions: **Champaran (1917)** against indigo cultivation, **Ahmedabad (1918)** for mill workers, **Kheda (1918)** against tax collection during crop failure. Each was local and limited — proving the method before scaling up.

**Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–22):** Triggered by the **Rowlatt Act** (1919) and the **Jallianwala Bagh massacre** (April 13, 1919 — General Dyer fired on peaceful crowd at Amritsar). Combined with the **Khilafat Movement** (Ali brothers supporting the Ottoman Caliph), it achieved unprecedented Hindu-Muslim unity. Gandhi abruptly withdrew after **Chauri Chaura (Feb 1922)** where a mob burned a police station killing 22 policemen.

**Civil Disobedience Movement (1930–34):** After the 1929 Lahore Session declared **Purna Swaraj** (complete independence) as the goal, Gandhi launched the **Dandi March** (March 12, 1930) — walking 241 miles to the sea to make salt, defying the British salt monopoly. The movement was suspended by the **Gandhi-Irwin Pact (1931)** and later resumed but suppressed by 1934.

**Quit India Movement (1942):** August 8, 1942, Gowalia Tank, Bombay — Gandhi's call: **"Do or Die."** The entire Congress leadership was arrested within hours. What followed was India's most spontaneous, leaderless rebellion — workers, students, and farmers attacking symbols of British authority across the country. The movement was brutally suppressed but demonstrated that the British could no longer govern India without Indian cooperation.

> 📌 **Exam Note:** **Subhas Chandra Bose** resigned from the INC presidency in 1939 (Tripuri session), formed the **Forward Bloc**, and later led the **Indian National Army (INA)** — "Give me blood, I will give you freedom." INA was originally formed by Mohan Singh; Bose reorganized and led it.

## The Andhra Connection

> 🏛️ **AP Exam Focus:** The **first English factory in India** was at **Masulipatnam (Machilipatnam), 1611** — in present-day Andhra Pradesh. This makes AP's history of colonial contact among the oldest in India.

**Alluri Sitarama Raju** led the **Rampa Rebellion (1922–24)** in the Godavari agency area against the Madras Forest Act that restricted tribal access to forests. He is celebrated as **Manyam Veerudu** (Hero of the Forests) in Telugu culture.

**Pingali Venkayya** of Machilipatnam designed the **Indian National Flag** — presenting early designs to Gandhi in 1921. This is a key AP question.

The **Vandemataram Movement** found strong resonance in Rajahmundry, where Bipin Chandra Pal's tour and student strikes at Rajahmundry Arts College marked early extremist nationalism in the Telugu region. The **Chirala-Perala movement** (led by Duggirala Gopalakrishnayya) was a local non-cooperation campaign against local taxation — a distinctly Andhra contribution.

## Connecting the Dots

The British succeeded in India because of **three structural advantages**: military superiority (artillery, disciplined infantry), financial extraction (Bengal's revenues funded further conquest), and political exploitation (playing Indian rulers against each other). The nationalist movement dismantled each of these: Gandhian nonviolence neutralized military superiority, the Drain Theory delegitimized financial extraction, and the INC's mass mobilization united communities the British had divided.

The Revolt of 1857 was a **turning point** — not because it succeeded, but because its failure taught nationalists that military revolt couldn't work. The constitutional path (Moderates), the cultural assertiveness path (Extremists), and the mass civil disobedience path (Gandhi) were successive responses to this lesson.
