# Union, States & Federal Structure

> *India chose to be a "Union of States" — not a federation. That one word reveals everything about how this country was designed: strong at the centre, flexible at the edges.*

**Covers:** G1 Prelims Unit 4 | G2 Paper 1 Section B | G1 Mains Paper 3
**Sources:** Constitution of India (Art 1–4, 245–255, 352–360), NCERT Class 11 (Federalism), AP SCERT History

## The Big Picture

When India became independent in 1947, the central political challenge was not writing a constitution — it was deciding whether India would hold together at all. With 562 princely states, multiple languages, deep religious divisions, and fresh memories of Partition, the framers faced an existential question: how do you create unity in such diversity?

Their answer was a federal system with a pronounced unitary bias. The Constitution distributes powers between the Centre and States, but tilts the balance firmly toward the Centre through Emergency provisions, the appointment of Governors, All-India Services, and residuary powers. This is not accidental. The framers feared fragmentation more than centralization.

For APPSC candidates, this topic is heavily tested through: the Articles defining the Union and territory (1–4), the Centre-State legislative relations (245–255), the reorganization of Andhra Pradesh, and the federal versus unitary debate.

## 1. The Union and Its Territory — Articles 1 to 4

These four Articles define what India is and how its territory can change:

| Article | Subject | Key Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Article 1** | India is a "Union of States" | Not "Federation" — states cannot secede. India is an **indestructible union of destructible states**. |
| **Article 2** | Admission or establishment of **new states** | Parliament can admit entirely new states (outside existing union) by simple majority |
| **Article 3** | Formation/alteration of **existing states** | Parliament can form new states from existing ones, change boundaries, or change names — by simple majority, **after** consulting affected state legislature |
| **Article 4** | Laws under Art 2 & 3 | These are NOT constitutional amendments under Art 368 — they require only simple majority |

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** Article 1 uses "Union of States" deliberately. It was a conscious choice by the framers (especially Dr. Ambedkar) to signal that India is NOT a compact among states. States cannot unilaterally leave. This is the legal foundation for why any secessionist movement is unconstitutional.

> 📌 **Exam Note:** When Parliament creates a new state from an existing one (Art 3), it must **consult** the state legislature — but is NOT bound by that opinion. It can override the state's wishes. This is another illustration of unitary bias.

## 2. Reorganisation of States — India and the AP Story

Post-independence, the demand for states based on linguistic identity was unstoppable. The Andhra Pradesh story is the most APPSC-relevant part of this history.

### The Timeline

| Event | Year | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Dhar Commission** | 1948 | Rejected linguistic basis for states; recommended administrative convenience |
| **JVP Committee** (Jawaharlal-Vallabhbhai-Pattabhi) | 1948–49 | Also rejected linguistic basis initially |
| **Potti Sreeramulu's fast** | 1952–53 | 56-day hunger strike demanding a separate Andhra state; died on 15 December 1952 |
| **Andhra State formed** | **1 October 1953** | First linguistic state in India; capital **Kurnool**; carved from Madras State |
| **States Reorganisation Commission (Fazal Ali)** | 1953–55 | Accepted language as a basis (with conditions); led to SRA 1956 |
| **States Reorganisation Act (SRA)** | **1 November 1956** | Created 14 states and 6 UTs on linguistic basis |
| **Gentlemen's Agreement** | 1956 | Agreement between Andhra and Hyderabad leaders for merger |
| **Andhra Pradesh formed** | **1 November 1956** | Merger of Andhra State + Hyderabad State (Telugu-speaking districts); Hyderabad as capital |
| **Telangana formed** | **2 June 2014** | Bifurcation of AP under AP Reorganisation Act 2014; 29th state of India |

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** The **Gentlemen's Agreement (1956)** was a crucial assurance given to Telangana leaders when merging Hyderabad State into Andhra Pradesh. It promised protections for Telangana in employment, education, and development. Its perceived violation was one of the driving grievances of the Telangana movement 1969 onwards.

> 📌 **Exam Note:** **Potti Sreeramulu** is a defining hero for APPSC. He is called the **"Amarajeevi"** (Immortal Soul) who sacrificed his life for a Telugu state. The Andhra Pradesh government has declared his death anniversary (15 December) as **Telugu Lovers' Day**.

## 3. Federal Structure — The Dual Character of Indian Polity

India has features of both a federal and a unitary system. The balance is not equal:

### Federal Features

| Feature | How it Works |
|---|---|
| **Dual Polity** | Two tiers of government — Centre and States — each supreme in their domain |
| **Written Constitution** | Powers defined in writing — neither Centre nor State can unilaterally alter the framework |
| **Division of Powers** | 7th Schedule: **Union List** (97 subjects, Centre only), **State List** (66 subjects, States only), **Concurrent List** (47 subjects, both; Centre prevails in conflict) |
| **Supremacy of Constitution** | Both Centre and States derive their powers from the Constitution |
| **Independent Judiciary** | Supreme Court adjudicates Centre-State disputes |

### Unitary Features (The Dominant Side)

| Feature | How it Works |
|---|---|
| **Residuary Powers with Centre** | Any subject not in any of the three lists goes to Parliament (not states, unlike USA) |
| **Single Citizenship** | Only Indian citizenship — no state citizenship (unlike USA, Canada) |
| **Appointment of Governor** | President (Central government) appoints and can dismiss State Governors |
| **All-India Services** | IAS, IPS, IFoS — officers serve both Centre and States, appointed by Centre |
| **Emergency Provisions** (Art 352–360) | During National Emergency (Art 352), State Emergency/President's Rule (Art 356), or Financial Emergency (Art 360) — federal structure collapses into unitary |
| **Parliament can legislate on State List** | Under Art 249 (Rajya Sabha passes resolution), Art 250 (during National Emergency), Art 252 (two or more states consent) |

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** This is why K.C. Wheare called India "quasi-federal" and Granville Austin called it a "cooperative federation." Neither pure federal nor pure unitary. The design gives Parliament overriding powers while allowing states significant autonomy in normal times. Emergency provisions are the key: they can convert the system temporarily into a unitary state.

## 4. Centre-State Legislative Relations (Articles 245–255)

| Article | Provision |
|---|---|
| **Art 245** | Parliament legislates for whole of India; State legislatures for the state |
| **Art 246** | Distribution based on Union, State, Concurrent Lists (7th Schedule) |
| **Art 249** | Rajya Sabha can authorize Parliament to legislate on State List (2/3rd majority) |
| **Art 254** | If Parliament and State law on Concurrent List conflict — **Parliament prevails** |

## 5. Centre-State Relations — Key Commissions

Two commissions are frequently asked in APPSC:

**Sarkaria Commission (1983):**
- Set up to review Centre-State relations
- Key recommendation: Article 356 (President's Rule) should be used **sparingly and as a last resort** — not as a political tool
- Also recommended more financial autonomy for states

**Punchhi Commission (2007):**
- Follow-up to Sarkaria
- Emphasized **Cooperative Federalism**
- Recommended that Governors be appointed in consultation with State Chief Ministers
- Warned against arbitrary use of discretionary powers by Governors

> 📌 **Exam Note:** The **National Development Council (NDC)** and **GST Council** are real-world examples of cooperative federalism — bodies where Centre and States jointly take decisions on planning and taxation. GST Council (Article 279A, added by 101st Amendment 2016) requires 75% majority, giving states significant say.

## 6. Parliamentary Form of Government

India's parliamentary system — distinct from presidential systems — means:

- **Executive responsible to Legislature**: PM and CoM hold office only while they command Lok Sabha majority. If they lose confidence vote, they must resign.
- **Collective Responsibility**: All ministers are collectively responsible to Lok Sabha. One minister's decision is the whole cabinet's decision.
- **Individual Responsibility**: Each minister is individually responsible to Parliament for their ministry's functioning.
- **Fusion of Powers**: Unlike USA where executive and legislature are strictly separate, in India the executive (Council of Ministers) is part of the legislature.

| Article | Subject |
|---|---|
| **Art 74 & 75** | Council of Ministers at Centre (aid and advice to President; collective responsibility to LS) |
| **Art 163 & 164** | Council of Ministers at State level (aid and advice to Governor) |

## Quick Revision — High-Frequency APPSC Facts

**Articles (Must-Know):**
- **Art 1**: India = Union of States (not Federation; no secession)
- **Art 2**: New states (outside union) admitted by Parliament
- **Art 3**: Existing state boundaries changed by Parliament (simple majority)
- **Art 249**: RS can authorize Parliament to legislate on State List
- **Art 356**: President's Rule (use sparingly per Sarkaria)
