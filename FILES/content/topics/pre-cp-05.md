# Rights Issues — Fundamental Rights, Human Rights & Protective Laws

> *Fundamental Rights are the Constitution's promises to every Indian citizen — enforceable promises, not aspirations. They are the difference between a rule of law and a rule of rulers.*

**Covers:** G1 Prelims Unit 4 | G2 Paper 1 Section B | G1 Mains Paper 3
**Sources:** Constitution of India (Part III), M. Laxmikanth, NHRC documentation, Supreme Court judgments

---

## The Big Picture

Part III of the Indian Constitution (Articles 12–35) is the Bill of Rights. It guarantees citizens enforceable protections against the state — and the Supreme Court has consistently expanded its scope through interpretation. Article 21 (Right to Life and Personal Liberty) began as a narrow provision and has been expanded by the courts to include the right to livelihood, health, education, privacy, a clean environment, and much more.

The critical distinction: **Fundamental Rights are justiciable** (enforceable in courts). **Directive Principles of State Policy** are not. If your Fundamental Right is violated, you can go directly to the Supreme Court under Article 32 — which Ambedkar called the "heart and soul of the Constitution."

## 1. Fundamental Rights — Overview (Articles 12–35)

Six categories of Fundamental Rights:

| Article(s) | Right | Key Provision |
|---|---|---|
| **14** | Right to Equality | Equality before law + Equal protection of the laws (positive obligation on state) |
| **15** | No Discrimination | On grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth; **Art 15(3)** allows special provisions for women & children |
| **16** | Equality of Opportunity in Public Employment | **Art 16(4)**: Reservation for backward classes; **Art 16(4A)**: Reservation in promotion for SC/ST |
| **17** | Abolition of Untouchability | Absolute — no exceptions, no reasonable restrictions |
| **19** | Six Freedoms | Speech & expression; peaceful assembly; association; movement; residence; profession/occupation |
| **20** | Protection against Conviction | No ex-post-facto laws; no double jeopardy; no self-incrimination |
| **21** | Right to Life & Personal Liberty | Most expansive FR — see below |
| **21A** | Right to Education | Free and compulsory for ages 6–14; added by 86th Amendment (2002) |
| **22** | Protection against Arrest | Right to be informed of grounds; right to a lawyer; 24-hour magistrate rule |
| **23** | Prohibition of Traffic in Persons | Begar (forced labour) and similar exploitation prohibited |
| **24** | Prohibition of Child Labour | No employment below 14 years in factories, mines, hazardous work |
| **25–28** | Freedom of Religion | Freedom of conscience; practise and propagate religion; religious instruction in state-aided schools |
| **29–30** | Cultural & Educational Rights | Minorities' right to conserve culture; right to establish educational institutions |
| **32** | Right to Constitutional Remedies | Move Supreme Court directly for enforcement; **Ambedkar: "heart and soul of the Constitution"** |

> 📌 **Exam Trap:** **Right to Property** was originally Article 31 (Fundamental Right). It was **removed** by the **44th Constitutional Amendment Act (1978)** and re-inserted as a **legal right** under **Article 300A**. Article 300A says property cannot be taken without authority of law — but it is NOT a Fundamental Right and cannot be enforced through Article 32.

### Article 21 — The Constitution's Most Expansive Right

The Supreme Court has used Article 21 to progressively expand what "life and liberty" means:

- **Right to Livelihood** (Olga Tellis, 1985)
- **Right to Education** (Mohini Jain, 1992 — before 21A was inserted)
- **Right to Health and Medical Care** (Paschim Banga, 1996)
- **Right to Privacy** (K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, **2017** — 9-judge bench)
- **Right to a Clean Environment** (Subhash Kumar, 1991)
- **Right against Solitary Confinement** (Sunil Batra, 1980)

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** The **Puttaswamy judgment (2017)** recognized **Privacy as a Fundamental Right** under Article 21. This has far-reaching implications — it forms the basis for challenging Aadhaar data collection, social media regulation, and state surveillance. It is one of the most significant constitutional judgments in decades.

## 2. Constitutional Remedies — The Writs (Article 32 & 226)

Five types of writs can be issued by the Supreme Court (Art 32) and High Courts (Art 226):

| Writ | Literal Meaning | Against Whom | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Habeas Corpus** | "Produce the body" | ANY authority (including private individuals) | Release of an illegally or unlawfully detained person |
| **Mandamus** | "We command" | Public authority | Compels performance of a statutory or public duty |
| **Prohibition** | Forbid | Lower courts and tribunals | Prevents a court/tribunal from exceeding its jurisdiction |
| **Certiorari** | "To be certified" | Lower courts and tribunals | Quashes an illegal or erroneous order already passed |
| **Quo Warranto** | "By what authority" | Public office holders | Questions a person's authority to hold a public office |

> 📌 **Exam Note:** **Habeas Corpus** is unique — it can be issued against **private individuals** too. All other writs lie primarily against public authorities. Also, **Prohibition** is prospective (prevents future action), while **Certiorari** is retrospective (quashes past orders). **High Courts** have a wider writ jurisdiction than the Supreme Court — they can also issue writs for purposes other than enforcing Fundamental Rights.

## 3. National Human Rights Commission (NHRC)

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Established** | **12 October 1993** — under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 |
| **Nature** | Statutory body, quasi-judicial — NOT a constitutional body |
| **Chairperson** | A retired Chief Justice of India |
| **Composition** | Chairperson + 5 members (retired CJI/SC judges + persons with knowledge of human rights) |
| **Jurisdiction** | Can investigate complaints of human rights violations by the state or its officials |
| **Limitation** | Cannot investigate matters older than **1 year** from the date of violation; cannot investigate armed forces violations (only by government request) |

> 📌 **Exam Note:** NHRC's recommendations are **NOT binding** on the government. It can only recommend compensation, remedial action, or prosecution. It cannot enforce its own recommendations. This is frequently the subject of criticism — and of APPSC questions.

**State Human Rights Commissions (SHRCs):**
- Established under the same Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993
- **AP State Human Rights Commission** is a key AP-specific fact

## 4. RTI Act (Right to Information), 2005

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Applies to** | All "public authorities" — bodies created under Constitution, law, or government notification; or substantially funded by government |
| **Response time** | Information must be provided within **30 days** |
| **Life/Liberty matters** | Information provided within **48 hours** |
| **Appellate mechanism** | First appeal: Senior officer in same organisation → Second appeal: **Central Information Commission (CIC)** / State Information Commission |
| **Exempt organisations** | Intelligence/security agencies listed in 2nd Schedule (IB, RAW, NTRO, etc.) — BUT not exempt for allegations of corruption or human rights violations |

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** RTI has been called the most powerful tool of grassroots democracy in India. It operationalized the right to information as a part of **Article 19(1)(a)** (freedom of speech and expression), per the Supreme Court's interpretation. However, the RTI (Amendment) Act 2019 changed the tenure and salaries of Information Commissioners — reducing their independence, which drew criticism.

## 5. Key Protective Laws — Quick Reference

### POCSO Act, 2012 (Protection of Children from Sexual Offences)

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Child defined** | Any person below **18 years** |
| **Gender-neutral** | Both boy and girl victims covered |
| **Special Courts** | Mandatory for speedy trial |
| **Burden of proof** | **Reversed** — accused presumed guilty once basic facts established; must prove innocence |
| **Mandatory reporting** | Anyone aware of an offence MUST report to police; failure to report is also an offence |

### SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Prevents atrocities against SC/ST communities; provides special courts |
| **Key 2018 Amendment** | Overturned SC judgment in *Subhash Kashinath Mahajan* case that had required prior sanction for arrest; restored immediate arrest provision |
| **Special Courts** | Exclusive special courts for speedy trial |
| **Nodal Ministry** | Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment |

### PWDVA, 2005 (Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act)

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Coverage** | Physical, sexual, verbal, emotional, **economic** abuse |
| **Wider than marriage** | Covers live-in relationships and women in "relationships in the nature of marriage" |
| **Mechanism** | Protection Officers appointed; Magistrate can issue Protection Orders, Residence Orders |

## Quick Revision — High-Frequency APPSC Facts

**Key Articles:**
- **Art 14**: Equality before law (also equal protection = positive right)
- **Art 15(3)**: Special provisions for women & children (allows positive discrimination)
- **Art 17**: Abolition of untouchability (absolute right)
- **Art 21**: Right to Life (includes privacy, livelihood, health, education)
- **Art 21A**: Right to Education 6–14 years (86th Amendment, 2002)
- **Art 24**: No child labour under 14 in hazardous employment
- **Art 32**: Right to Constitutional Remedies (Ambedkar: "heart and soul")
- **Art 300A**: Right to property (legal right, NOT Fundamental Right, since 44th Amendment 1978)

**NHRC Key Facts:**
- Established: **12 October 1993**
- Nature: **Statutory** (NOT constitutional)
- Chairman: **Retired Chief Justice of India**
- Limitation: Cannot investigate violations older than **1 year**
- Recommendations: **NOT binding**

**Writs:**
- **Habeas Corpus**: Against anyone (including private persons); release from illegal detention
- **Mandamus**: Compels public duty
- **Quo Warranto**: Questions authority to hold office
- **Prohibition** (prospective) vs. **Certiorari** (retrospective)

**POCSO:**
- Child = below **18 years**
- Burden of proof **reversed** (on accused)
- Mandatory reporting — failure to report is also an offence
