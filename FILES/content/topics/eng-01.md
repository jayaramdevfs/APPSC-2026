# English Communication Skills

> *The ability to write clearly is the ability to think clearly. A civil servant who cannot express a decision in plain English cannot be held accountable for it — which is why writing is not a soft skill. It is the core skill.*

**Covers:** G1 Language Paper (English) | G2 Language Paper (English Section)
**Sources:** APPSC English Syllabus; Oxford Guide to Plain English; Wren & Martin English Grammar

## The Big Picture

The English section of APPSC tests functional English skills — writing that communicates clearly and professionally, comprehension that extracts meaning accurately, and grammar that ensures precision. The tests are practical, not literary: can you write a report, a letter, or a précis? Can you understand a passage and answer questions accurately? Can you identify errors and use words correctly?

This note covers the four main areas: Essay Writing, Précis Writing, Official Writing (letters and reports), Reading Comprehension, and Grammar essentials for competitive exams.

## 1. Essay Writing

### Structure of an Essay

A good competitive exam essay has three parts:

| Part | Content | Length (approx.) |
|------|---------|------------------|
| **Introduction** | Hook + context + thesis statement (your main argument) | 10–15% |
| **Body** | 3–5 paragraphs; each paragraph = one main point + evidence + analysis | 70–80% |
| **Conclusion** | Restate thesis differently; implications; forward-looking statement | 10–15% |

### Types of Essays

| Type | Characteristics | Example Topics |
|------|----------------|----------------|
| **Argumentative** | Takes a clear position; presents evidence for and against; concludes with reasoned verdict | "Technology is doing more harm than good" |
| **Descriptive** | Describes a subject vividly; appeals to senses | "A day in a village" |
| **Narrative** | Tells a story; personal or fictional | "An experience that changed me" |
| **Expository** | Explains a concept, process, or situation objectively | "How RTI has changed governance" |
| **Analytical** | Breaks down a complex issue into components | "Causes of farmer distress in India" |

### Writing a Strong Paragraph

**PEEL structure** for body paragraphs:
- **P**oint — State your main idea
- **E**vidence — Provide specific facts, examples, or data
- **E**xplain — Show how the evidence supports your point
- **L**ink — Connect to the essay's thesis or next paragraph

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** In competitive exam essays, **specificity beats generality**. "India faces environmental challenges" is weak. "India loses ₹3.75 lakh crore annually to air pollution health costs (World Bank, 2020)" is strong. Examiners reward candidates who demonstrate they know actual facts, not just abstract principles. Use data, names, dates, and examples — they demonstrate preparation.

### Essay Writing Tips for APPSC

1. **Plan before writing** — spend 5 minutes making a point-outline
2. **One idea per paragraph** — do not mix multiple arguments
3. **Vary sentence length** — mix short punchy sentences with longer analytical ones
4. **Avoid vague words** — "things", "stuff", "very", "a lot" weaken writing
5. **Active voice** — "The government implemented the scheme" (not "The scheme was implemented by the government")
6. **Start strong** — begin with a striking fact, quote, or question

## 2. Précis Writing

**Précis** (from French: precise, exact) is the art of condensing a passage to about **1/3 of its original length** while retaining all essential ideas.

### Rules for Précis Writing

| Rule | Detail |
|------|--------|
| **Length** | Approximately **1/3 of original** (if passage = 300 words, précis ≈ 100 words) |
| **Own words** | Use your own words; do not copy sentences from passage |
| **Third person** | Write in third person (even if original uses first person) |
| **Past tense** | Usually write in past tense |
| **Complete sentences** | Proper sentences; no bullet points |
| **No opinions** | Do not add your own views; only capture what the original says |
| **No examples** | If original gives examples to illustrate a point, summarize the point — drop the examples |
| **Title** | Give a brief, apt title at the top |

### Process

1. Read the passage twice — once for general meaning, once for details
2. Identify the **main idea** and **supporting ideas**
3. Note key points (in margins or rough paper)
4. Write the précis — without looking at the original if possible
5. Count words; adjust to approximately 1/3
6. Read once more to check: nothing essential missed? Nothing added that wasn't there?

> 📌 **Exam Note:** The most common error in précis writing is **keeping examples** that the original used to illustrate a principle. The principle is essential; the example is not. If a passage uses three examples to support one point, your précis should capture the point, not any of the examples.

## 3. Official Letter Writing

### Types of Official Letters

| Type | Purpose | Format |
|------|---------|--------|
| **Letter to an official** | Formal request, complaint, or information to a government officer | D.O. Letter (Demi-Official) or official |
| **Letter to the editor** | Opinion on a public issue; formal but not bureaucratic | Formal letter format |
| **Application letter** | Applying for a job, permission, or benefit | Formal application format |
| **Notice** | Announcing an event or regulation | Headed notice format |
| **Circular** | Mass communication within or from an organization | Circular format |

### Format of a Formal Letter

```
[Sender's Address]
[Date]

[Recipient's Name]
[Designation]
[Organization]
[Address]

Subject: [Clear subject line — no more than one line]

Sir/Madam,

[Opening paragraph: Purpose of writing — state it immediately]

[Body paragraph(s): Details, explanations, supporting information]

[Closing paragraph: What action is expected; deadline if any]

Yours faithfully/sincerely,
[Signature]
[Name in capitals]
[Designation]
```

**Yours faithfully** = when you do NOT know the person's name (Sir/Madam)
**Yours sincerely** = when you DO know the person's name (Dear Mr. Sharma)

> 📌 **Exam Note:** The salutation–closing pairing is strictly tested:
> - "Dear Sir/Madam" → "Yours faithfully"
> - "Dear Mr./Mrs. [Name]" → "Yours sincerely"
> Using "Yours faithfully" after "Dear Mr. Sharma" or "Yours sincerely" after "Dear Sir" is an error.

## 4. Report Writing

### Structure of an Official Report

| Section | Content |
|---------|---------|
| **Title** | Report on [subject]; submitted by [name]; date |
| **Terms of Reference (ToR)** | Why the report was commissioned; what questions it addresses |
| **Procedure/Methodology** | How information was gathered |
| **Findings** | What was discovered — organized with headings |
| **Conclusions** | What the findings mean |
| **Recommendations** | What should be done |
| **Appendices** | Supporting documents, tables, lists |

**Note-writing** (for administrative files):
- Read all previous papers on the subject
- State the issue clearly and concisely
- List options
- Recommend an action with reasons
- End with "Submitted for orders" or "For approval"

## 5. Reading Comprehension — Strategies

### Approach

1. **Skim** the passage (30 seconds) — get the topic and general structure
2. **Read questions** before reading carefully — know what to look for
3. **Read carefully** — underline key facts, dates, names
4. **Answer from the passage** — do not use outside knowledge; answer only what the passage says

### Types of Questions

| Type | Strategy |
|------|---------|
| **Factual** | Find specific information; underline while reading |
| **Inference** | What does the passage imply but not say directly? Use context |
| **Vocabulary** | What does the word mean in this context? Use surrounding sentences |
| **Main idea** | What is the passage primarily about? Usually in opening or closing sentences |
| **Title** | What title best fits? Must capture the central theme, not a detail |

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** In comprehension, the most important rule is **answer from the passage**. Your prior knowledge may conflict with what the passage says — always go with the passage. If the passage says "the moon is made of green cheese," the correct answer to "What is the moon made of?" is "green cheese." Examiners test comprehension, not knowledge.

## 6. Grammar Essentials

### Common Grammar Errors (high-frequency in competitive exams)

**Subject-Verb Agreement:**
- "The committee **has** decided" (not have — committee = singular)
- "Neither the manager nor the employees **were** present" (verb agrees with nearest subject)
- "Each of the students **has** a book" (each = singular)

**Tense Usage:**
- Present Perfect: "She **has lived** here since 2010" (from then to now; use "since" + point in time)
- Simple Past: "She **lived** here in 2010" (specific past time)
- Never: "She is living here since 2010" (mixing present continuous and since = wrong)

**Prepositions (common errors):**

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| discuss about | discuss (no "about") |
| similar with | similar to |
| different than | different from |
| marry with | marry (no preposition) |
| comprise of | comprise OR consist of |

**Articles (a/an/the):**
- "a" before consonant sounds: a university (yoo-niversity = consonant sound)
- "an" before vowel sounds: an hour (silent h; vowel sound)
- "the" for specific/unique: the Prime Minister; the Ganga

**Commonly Confused Words:**

| Word | Meaning | Word | Meaning |
|------|---------|------|---------|
| affect (verb) | to influence | effect (noun) | result |
| accept | to receive | except | excluding |
| advice (noun) | guidance | advise (verb) | to counsel |
| principal | head/main | principle | rule/value |
| stationary | not moving | stationery | writing materials |
| complement | complete | compliment | praise |
| elicit | to draw out | illicit | illegal |
| emigrate | leave a country | immigrate | enter a country |

> 📌 **Exam Note:** **"Affect vs. Effect"** and **"Principal vs. Principle"** are the most frequently tested confused word pairs in competitive exams. Also note: "comprise" is often misused — "The committee comprises five members" (not "is comprised of"). "Consist of" is the correct phrase with "of": "The committee consists of five members."

### Active vs. Passive Voice

| Active | Passive |
|--------|---------|
| The officer signed the order. | The order was signed by the officer. |
| Use for: direct, clear, formal writing | Use for: when actor is unknown, unimportant, or to be de-emphasized |
| Preferred in most contexts | Overuse = weak writing |

### Direct vs. Indirect Speech

| Direct | Indirect |
|--------|---------|
| He said, "I am tired." | He said that he was tired. |
| She said, "I will come tomorrow." | She said that she would come the next day. |
| Changes: tense shifts back (am → was; will → would); pronouns shift (I → he); time words change (today → that day; tomorrow → next day) |

## Quick Revision — High-Frequency APPSC Facts

**Essay:** Introduction (thesis) → Body (PEEL paragraphs) → Conclusion; specific > general; active voice; one idea per paragraph

**Précis:** 1/3 of original length; own words; third person; past tense; no opinions; no examples; apt title

**Letter format:**
- "Dear Sir" / "Dear Madam" → **Yours faithfully**
- "Dear Mr. Sharma" → **Yours sincerely**

**Comprehension rule:** Answer FROM the passage; do not use outside knowledge

**Key grammar rules:**
- Subject-verb agreement: singular subjects take singular verbs (each, either, neither, committee)
- "Since" + point in time → Present Perfect tense
- Affect (verb) ≠ Effect (noun)
- Principal (main/head) ≠ Principle (rule/value)
- "Comprise" takes no "of"; "consist" takes "of"
- "Different from" (not "different than")
- "Discuss" takes no "about"

**Voice:** Prefer active for clarity; passive for depersonalizing or when actor unknown
