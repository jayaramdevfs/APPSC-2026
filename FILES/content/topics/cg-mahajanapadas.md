## Unit 1: Conditions for the Rise of Large Territorial States

The transformation from small tribal units (*janas*) to large territorial kingdoms (*mahajanapadas*) was not sudden — it was driven by a set of material conditions that matured together around the sixth century BC.

**Iron and Agriculture:** From the sixth century BC onwards, the increasing use of iron in eastern UP and western Bihar created conditions for large territorial states. Iron tools and ploughshares allowed peasants to clear heavy jungle land and cultivate far more than before. The surplus food grain that resulted could be collected by princes to meet their military and administrative needs. Iron weapons, in turn, strengthened the warrior class and allowed kings to project power over wider territories.

**The Shift from Jana to Janapada:** In the early Vedic period, people felt primary loyalty to their *jana* (tribe or clan). By the sixth century BC, this had shifted — people felt loyalty to the *janapada*, the territory to which they belonged. This territorial identity was crucial: it meant that a king could claim allegiance from all inhabitants of a region, not just members of a particular tribe. The *mahajanapadas* grew from this territorial consciousness.

**Urban Centres:** The surplus from iron-tool agriculture also fed the towns that sprang up in the fifth century BC — the Second Urbanization. These towns provided markets, artisan production, and trade routes. Kings who controlled towns could levy taxes on trade and accumulate the wealth needed to maintain professional armies.

**Summary of conditions:**
- Iron tools → agricultural surplus → state revenue
- Iron weapons → powerful warrior class → military expansion
- Territorial loyalty (janapada) replaced tribal loyalty (jana)
- Towns and trade → taxable commerce → state finance

## Unit 2: The Sixteen Mahajanapadas

*Janapadas* (territorial units) began to appear at the end of the Vedic period. With the progress of agriculture and settled life by 500 BC, they became common. Around 450 BC, the grammarian Panini mentions over forty *janapadas* covering even Afghanistan and south-eastern Central Asia. The Pali Buddhist texts mention sixteen large states called *mahajanapadas* in the age of the Buddha.

Most of the sixteen were situated in the upper and mid-Gangetic plains — the doab region between the Ganges and Yamuna and their tributaries — mostly north of the Vindhyas, extending from the north-west frontier to Bihar. The major part of southern India was excluded.

**The four most powerful were: Magadha, Koshala, Vatsa, and Avanti.**

### Key Mahajanapadas — One by One:

**1. Anga**
- Covered modern Monghyr and Bhagalpur districts
- Capital: Champa — shows signs of habitation in the fifth century BC; a mud fort dates to that century
- Eventually absorbed by its powerful neighbour Magadha

**2. Magadha**
- Covered former districts of Patna, Gaya, and parts of Shahabad
- Two capitals: earlier at **Rajgir** (called Girivraja), later at **Pataliputra** (modern Patna)
- Grew to become the dominant state and the base for India's first empire

**3. Vajji (including the Licchhavis)**
- Located north of the Ganges in the Tirhut division
- Included eight clans; the most powerful was the **Licchhavi** clan
- Capital: **Vaishali** (coterminous with Basarh village, Vaishali district)
- A *gana-sangha* (republican oligarchy) — not a monarchy
- Archaeologically, Basarh was not settled until the sixth century BC, despite Puranic claims of earlier antiquity
- The Vajji confederacy had a remarkable political structure: 7,707 Licchhavi rajas, with positions including raja, uparaja, senapati, bhandagarika

**4. Kashi**
- Capital: **Varanasi**
- Excavations at Rajghat show earliest habitation around 500 BC; city enclosed by mud embankments at the same time
- Initially the most powerful of the states; eventually absorbed by Koshala

**5. Koshala**
- Occupied eastern UP
- Capital: **Shravasti** (= Sahet-Mahet, on the borders of Gonda and Bahraich districts of UP)
- Barely settled in the sixth century BC; shows beginnings of a mud fort
- Had an important city called Ayodhya (associated with the *Ramayana*) but excavations show it was not settled before the fifth century BC
- Also included the tribal republican territory of the **Shakyas** at Kapilavastu (= Piprahwa, Basti district; habitation not before c.500 BC)
- **Lumbini**, birthplace of Gautama Buddha, was 15 km from Piprahwa in Nepal — confirmed by an Ashokan inscription

**6. Mallas**
- A republican clan in the neighbourhood of Koshala
- Their territory touched the northern border of the Vajji state
- Capital at **Kushinara** (= Kasia, Deoria district, eastern UP) — where Gautama Buddha passed away

**7. Vatsa**
- Along the Yamuna, capital at **Kaushambi** (near Allahabad)
- The Vatsas were a Kuru clan who had shifted from Hastinapur to Kaushambi — chosen for its strategic position at the confluence of the Ganga and Yamuna
- Had a mud fortification by the fifth century BC (excavations confirm)

**8. Avanti**
- In central Malwa and adjoining parts of MP
- Divided into two parts:
  - Northern capital: **Ujjain**
  - Southern capital: **Mahishmati**
- Ujjain eventually surpassed Mahishmati; developed large-scale iron working; erected strong fortifications
- Magadha's most serious rival — it took Magadha about a hundred years to subjugate Ujjain

**9. Kurus and Panchalas**
- In western UP
- Had been dominant during the later Vedic period (Kuru-Panchala was the heartland of later Vedic culture)
- By the age of Buddha, they no longer enjoyed the political significance they once had

**10. Gandhara and Kamboja (North-Western Mahajanapadas)**
- **Gandhara**: In the north-west (modern Pakistan/Afghanistan) — important strategically; faced Iranian and Alexander's invasions
- **Kamboja**: Located in Central Asia in the Pamir area (largely modern Tajikistan)
  - Remains of horse, chariots, spoked wheels, cremation, and svastika found there — associated with Indo-Aryan speakers dated 1500–1000 BC
  - Around 500 BC both Sanskrit and Pali were spoken in Kamboja
  - Connected with Pataliputra by the *uttarapatha* (northern high road)
  - Called a *janapada* by Panini but a *mahajanapada* in Pali texts

**Political dynamics:** From the sixth century BC onwards, these states engaged in constant struggle for supremacy. Eventually Magadha emerged as the most powerful and founded India's first empire.

## Unit 3: Second Urbanization — The World These States Lived In

The Mahajanapadas arose alongside a major transformation in material life — the Second Urbanization. (The first had been the Harappan; there was a 1,500-year gap between them.)

**NBPW Phase:** The material marker of this age is the **Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW)** — a lustrous, highly fired black pottery found at about 700 sites across north India. NBPW appears from roughly the 7th–5th centuries BC and is concentrated in the Gangetic plains.

**Punch-Marked Coins:** The first coins appeared in this period — **punch-marked coins** made of silver and copper. Marks on them include hills, trees, fish, bulls, elephants, and crescents. The earliest coin hoards are from eastern UP and Magadha. Pataliputra had a population of approximately 270,000 by 300 BC. There were 18 guilds (*shrenis*) of merchants and artisans.

**Agriculture:** Rice transplantation (*shali*) began in Buddha's age. The tax on produce was 1/6 (*bhaga*). The old voluntary *bali* (tribute) became compulsory. A new social figure emerged — the *gahapati* (rich peasant/householder), who owned land and hired labour.

**Twenty towns:** A Pali text speaks of twenty towns in the age of the Buddha. Most were in the mid-Gangetic plains and included Kaushambi, Shravasti, Varanasi, Vaishali, and Rajgriha.

## Unit 4: Jainism and Buddhism — Responses to the New Age

The sixth–fifth centuries BC saw a religious revolution. The material changes — iron ploughshares, surplus agriculture, towns, coins, inequality — created social tensions. New teachers arose to address them.

### Vardhamana Mahavira and Jainism

- Born 540 BC (one tradition); died **468 BC** at age 72 at **Pavapuri** near modern Rajgir
- Alternative tradition: born 599 BC, died 527 BC — but archaeology does not support 6th century BC: the towns associated with him did not come into existence until 500 BC
- 24th *tirthankara* (ford-crosser)

**Five Doctrines of Jainism:**
1. Do not commit violence (*ahimsa*)
2. Do not tell a lie
3. Do not steal
4. Do not hoard
5. Observe continence (*brahmacharya*) — the 5th doctrine was added by Mahavira; the first four came from his predecessor Parshva

**Key features:**
- *Ahimsa* was supreme — even agriculture was prohibited (it involves killing living beings) → Jainas confined themselves to trade and commerce
- *Triratna* (three jewels of Jainism): right knowledge + right faith + right action
- Liberation through these three — no ritual needed

**Two sects:**
- *Shvetambaras* — wore white garments
- *Digambaras* — remained naked
- Split formalized after a famine in Magadha ~200 years after Mahavira's death → migration south under Bhadrabahu; council at Pataliputra convened; southerners boycotted it

**Contribution of Jainism:**
- First serious attempt to mitigate evils of the varna order
- Adopted Prakrit → Shauraseni developed → Marathi grew from it
- Religious literature in Ardhamagadhi (compiled in 6th century AD at Valabhi, Gujarat)
- Contributed to Kannada literature; contributed to art and architecture in medieval times
- Jainism prohibited war and agriculture → Jainas became the dominant trading community

### Gautama Buddha and Buddhism

- Born **567 BC** (tradition) in a **Shakya kshatriya family** at **Lumbini**, Nepal
- Father = elected ruler of Kapilavastu; headed the Shakya republican clan
- Left home at 29; attained enlightenment at 35 at **Bodh-Gaya** under a pipal tree
- First sermon at **Sarnath**, Banaras
- Died at 80 in **487 BC** at **Kusinagara** (= Kasia, Deoria district, eastern UP)
- Note: cities he visited did not assume urban character until the fifth century BC

**Doctrines:**
- World full of sorrows; desires cause suffering → conquer desires → attain *nirvana*
- **Eightfold path (*ashtangika marga*):** right observation, determination, speech, action, livelihood, effort, awareness, concern
- **Middle path:** avoid excess of both luxury and austerity
- **Three Jewels:** Buddha + Dhamma + Sangha

**Why Buddhism spread:**
- Did not recognize god or soul — attacked varna system; admitted women and shudras to sangha
- Used Pali — language of common people (from ~500 BC)
- Particularly appealed to Magadha — placed outside Aryavarta; people looked down upon by orthodox Brahmanas
- Ashoka's patronage spread it to Central Asia, West Asia, Sri Lanka → world religion

**Decline by 12th century AD:**
- Monks abandoned Pali for Sanskrit; idol worship; rich offerings; monastery corruption
- Persecution by Pushyamitra Shunga, Huna Mihirakula, Shaivite Shashanka
- Turkish invaders destroyed Bihar monasteries; monks fled to Nepal and Tibet

## Unit 5: Rise and Growth of the Magadhan Empire

### Haryanka Dynasty

**Bimbisara (544–492 BC)**

Magadha came into real prominence under Bimbisara of the Haryanka dynasty — a contemporary of both Mahavira and the Buddha. He began the policy of conquest and aggression that culminated in Ashoka's Kalinga war, 300 years later.

- Conquered **Anga** and placed it under viceroyalty of son Ajatashatru at Champa
- **Three wives:**
  1. Daughter of the Koshala king → dowry: Kashi village yielding revenue of 100,000 (revenues in coins — marks the monetized economy)
  2. **Chellana** (Licchhavi princess from Vaishali) → gave birth to Ajatashatru
  3. Daughter of Madra clan chief (Punjab)
- Sent physician **Jivaka** to Ujjain when Avanti's king Chanda Pradyota fell ill with jaundice — diplomacy converting an enemy into a partner
- Capital: **Rajgir (Girivraja)** — surrounded by five hills; stone walls closed the openings → impregnable
- Ruled ~52 years (544–492 BC); succeeded by son Ajatashatru who **killed his father** and seized the throne

**Ajatashatru (492–460 BC)**

- High watermark of the Haryanka dynasty
- **War with Koshala:** Aggressive expansion provoked Kashi + Koshala combination; after prolonged conflict, Ajatashatru won; Koshalan king gave his daughter in marriage and ceded Kashi
- **War with Vaishali:** Licchhavis were allies of Koshala; Ajatashatru sowed dissension in their ranks; invaded; took **16 years** to end Licchhavi independence
  - New weapons: **catapult** (hurling stones) and **mace-chariot** (mass killings in field)
  - Added Kashi + Vaishali to Magadhan empire
- Began fortification of Rajgir against Avanti threat (remains still visible today)

**Udayin (460–444 BC)**

- Built fort at confluence of **Ganges and Son at Patna** → founded **Pataliputra**
- Patna lay at the centre of Magadhan kingdom (now extending from Himalayas to Chhotanagpur hills)
- This decision proved strategically crucial for centuries

---

### Shishunaga Dynasty (~412–344 BC)

- Greatest achievement: destroyed the power of **Avanti** (Ujjain) → ended the 100-year Magadha–Avanti rivalry
- Avanti absorbed into Magadhan empire permanently (until end of Maurya rule)
- Temporarily moved capital to Vaishali

---

### Nanda Dynasty (~344–322 BC)

The most powerful rulers Magadha had seen.

- **Mahapadma Nanda** — claimed title ***ekarat*** (sole sovereign; destroyer of all other ruling princes)
- Conquered **Kalinga** — brought image of *jina* as victory trophy
- Also captured Koshala
- Army: **200,000 infantry, 60,000 cavalry, 3,000–6,000 war elephants**
- Maintained through effective taxation system
- **Alexander dared not advance eastward** — his army refused, fearing this force on the Ganges
- Later Nandas proved weak and unpopular → supplanted by Chandragupta Maurya (322 BC)

## Unit 6: Causes of Magadha's Success

### 1. Iron Advantage
The richest iron deposits were near Rajgir (earliest capital). Ready iron → superior weapons → rivals at a disadvantage. Avanti also had iron (eastern MP mines near Ujjain) — which is exactly why it was Magadha's toughest rival and took 100 years to subdue.

### 2. Strategic Capitals
- **Rajgir**: Five surrounding hills → impregnable before cannon
- **Pataliputra**: At confluence of Ganges + Gandak + Son (+ nearby Ghaghra) → *jaladurga* (water fort); Ganges/Son on north/west; Poonpun on south/east; army could move in all directions via rivers; commands all communications

### 3. River Transport and Timber
Ganges = highway + agricultural support. Timber abundant (wooden palisades found south of Patna; Megasthenes described wooden walls). Boats easy to manufacture → projected power east and west.

### 4. Fertile Alluvium
Heavy rainfall + cleared jungle alluvium = immensely fertile land. Varieties of paddy. Far more productive than areas west of Allahabad. Surplus taxable.

### 5. Towns and Money
20 towns in age of Buddha (Pali text). Trade + tolls on commodities + metal money (punch-marked coins) = fiscal base for professional armies.

### 6. Elephant Warfare
Eastern India supplied war elephants. Nandas maintained 6,000. Elephants stormed fortresses and crossed marshy terrain impassable to cavalry and chariots — a decisive asymmetric advantage.

### 7. Unorthodox Social Character
Magadha was inhabited by Kiratas and Magadhas — outside orthodox Aryavarta. Recently Vedicized, hence more dynamic and expansionist than older kingdoms long under Vedic orthodoxy.

## Unit 7: Iranian and Macedonian Invasions

### Iranian (Achaemenian) Invasion

NW India in the 6th century BC: small principalities (Kambojas, Gandharas, Madras) fighting each other — no unifying power.

- **Darius I** (Achaemenian ruler of Iran) penetrated NW India in **516 BC**
- Annexed Punjab (west of Indus) + Sindh → converted into **20th satrapy** of Iran (28 total)
- Indian satrapy paid tribute of **360 talents of gold = one-third of Iran's entire Asian revenue**
- Indian soldiers enrolled in Iranian army; Xerxes used them against the Greeks
- India remained part of Iranian empire until Alexander's invasion

**Impact of Iranian contact:**
- **Kharoshthi script** introduced by Iranian scribes — used in NW India from 3rd century BC to 3rd century AD; written **right to left**
- Cultural contacts between Iran and India facilitated

### Alexander's Invasion (326 BC)

- Entered through **Khyber Pass** in 326 BC
- Defeated **Porus** (Paurava) on the **Jhelum** river
- Stayed 19 months (326–325 BC); army refused to advance further east (feared Nanda empire)
- Retreated; died in Babylon 323 BC

**Impact:**
- Opened India–Greek contact; Greek accounts (Megasthenes) describe Indian society
- Fragmented NW principalities → path cleared for Chandragupta Maurya to unify north India
- Gandhara art (Greek + Indian blend) emerged in subsequent centuries
