## The Big Picture

Ethics is the branch of philosophy that studies moral principles — how we determine right from wrong, good from evil. For civil services, ethics is not abstract philosophy but practical guidance: how should a government official act when duties conflict, when rules clash with conscience, or when authority is misused?

Three major ethical traditions answer this question differently: Consequentialism says judge actions by results; Deontology says judge actions by principles regardless of results; Virtue Ethics says judge actions by the character of the actor. Understanding all three — and their limits — is what the APPSC ethics paper tests.

## 1. Ethical Theories — The Three Major Schools

### Consequentialism — The Ethics of Outcomes

**Core idea:** The moral worth of an action is determined by its **consequences**. Right action = action that produces the best outcomes.

**Jeremy Bentham — Utilitarianism (1789)**

Bentham's *Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789) founded classical utilitarianism:
- **Pleasure principle (Hedonism)**: Humans are governed by pleasure and pain
- **Greatest Happiness Principle**: The right action is that which produces the **greatest happiness for the greatest number**
- **Felicific Calculus**: Happiness can be measured and calculated — intensity, duration, certainty, extent
- **Quantitative utilitarianism**: All pleasures are equal — only quantity matters

**John Stuart Mill — Refined Utilitarianism (1863)**

Mill modified Bentham in *Utilitarianism* (1863):
- **Qualitative utilitarianism**: Higher pleasures (intellectual, moral) are better than lower pleasures (physical)
- Famous quote: "It is better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied"
- Added **rule utilitarianism**: Follow rules that generally maximize utility, not just case-by-case calculation

**Criticisms of Consequentialism:**
- Justifies terrible means for good ends (torture an innocent to save many?)
- Minorities can be sacrificed for majority benefit
- Future consequences are unknowable — how do you calculate them?

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** The **"Trolley Problem"** is consequentialism's classic dilemma: pull a lever to divert a runaway trolley — it kills 1 person instead of 5. The consequentialist says yes (5 > 1). The deontologist says no (killing is wrong regardless). Real governance is full of trolley problems — spending on highways that benefit millions but displace hundreds. Consequentialism offers clarity; deontology offers constraints.

### Deontology — The Ethics of Duty

**Core idea:** Actions are intrinsically right or wrong based on their **nature**, not their consequences. Some actions are duties — obligatory regardless of outcome.

**Immanuel Kant — Categorical Imperative (1785)**

Kant's *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) is the foundation of modern deontology:

**The Categorical Imperative** (3 formulations, same principle different ways):

| Formulation | Statement |
|-------------|---------|
| **Universal Law Formula** | "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law." |
| **Humanity Formula** | "Act so that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or that of another, always as an end and never as a means only." |
| **Kingdom of Ends** | "Act according to maxims of a universally legislating member of a merely possible kingdom of ends." |

**What does this mean practically?**
- Lying is always wrong — even to a murderer asking where your friend is — because if everyone lied when convenient, communication would collapse (universal law fails)
- Treating people as means (using them as tools) is always wrong — even for good ends
- Moral worth comes from **duty and intention**, not results

**Key Kantian concepts:**
- **Categorical imperative**: Unconditional moral command (vs. hypothetical imperatives — "if you want X, do Y")
- **Good will**: The only thing unconditionally good; doing right because it is right (not for reward)
- **Dignity**: Every person has inherent worth and must be treated as an end

> 📌 **Exam Note:** Kant's **categorical imperative** is his most famous contribution and appears in almost every ethics exam. The key phrase is **"universal law"** — could this principle be universalized? Also remember the **humanity formula** — never treat people as mere means. In public administration context: a corrupt official treats citizens as means (extracting bribe) — direct violation of Kant's humanity formula.

**W.D. Ross — Prima Facie Duties**

Ross's *The Right and the Good* (1930) offered a moderate deontology:
- Multiple duties exist simultaneously: fidelity, reparation, gratitude, non-maleficence, beneficence, justice, self-improvement
- **Prima facie duties**: Each is binding unless overridden by a stronger duty in the specific situation
- More realistic than Kant — acknowledges that duties can conflict

### Virtue Ethics — The Ethics of Character

**Core idea:** Focus on the **character of the moral agent**, not rules or consequences. The virtuous person naturally does the right thing.

**Aristotle — Eudaimonia and Virtue (4th century BCE)**

Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* is the foundation:
- **Eudaimonia** (flourishing/happiness): The ultimate goal of human life
- Achieved through **virtue (arete)** — excellence of character
- **The Golden Mean**: Every virtue is the mean between two extremes (vices)
  - Courage = mean between cowardice and recklessness
  - Generosity = mean between miserliness and profligacy
  - Justice = mean between taking too much and too little

| Extreme (Vice) | Virtue (Mean) | Extreme (Vice) |
|----------------|--------------|----------------|
| Cowardice | **Courage** | Recklessness |
| Miserliness | **Generosity** | Extravagance |
| Obsequiousness | **Friendliness** | Quarrelsomeness |
| Excessive modesty | **Proper pride** | Vanity |

**Development of virtue:**
- Virtues are acquired through **habit and practice** (not innate)
- "We become just by doing just acts, brave by doing brave acts"
- The virtuous person doesn't struggle with temptation — virtue becomes second nature

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** Virtue ethics is increasingly influential in civil service ethics because it focuses on **who the official IS** rather than just what rules they follow. A rule-follower can find loopholes; a virtuous person has integrity. The **Nolan Principles** of public life (selflessness, integrity, objectivity, accountability, openness, honesty, leadership) are essentially a virtue-ethics framework for public officials.

## 2. Gandhian Ethics

Gandhi's ethical thought was action-based, not abstract philosophy:

| Concept | Content |
|---------|---------|
| **Ahimsa (Non-violence)** | Non-violence in thought, word, and deed; extends to all living beings; NOT mere passivity |
| **Satyagraha (Truth-force)** | Force of truth and love as political and ethical instrument; "truth is God" |
| **Sarvodaya** | Welfare of all; inspired by John Ruskin's *Unto This Last*; no one is an enemy |
| **Trusteeship** | The wealthy are trustees of society's wealth, not owners; wealth should be used for social good |
| **Antyodaya** | Welfare of the last person (*antyaja*) first; policy must help the most marginalized |
| **Swadeshi** | Local self-sufficiency; ethical consumption; buy what your community produces |

> 📌 **Exam Note:** Gandhi's **Antyodaya** — "uplift of the last person" — directly influenced India's development planning philosophy and is embedded in the Preamble's idea of social justice. It is conceptually different from Sarvodaya (welfare of ALL) — Antyodaya specifically means prioritizing the LAST, the most deprived. This distinction appears in MCQs.

### Gandhi vs. Bentham: A Contrast

| Dimension | Bentham | Gandhi |
|-----------|---------|--------|
| Foundation | Pleasure/Pain | Truth/Non-violence |
| Method | Calculation | Conscience |
| View of people | Pleasure-seekers | Soul-bearers |
| Means | Justify by ends | Means ARE the end (inseparable) |

Gandhi explicitly rejected Bentham's view that ends justify means — "the means are the end in the making."

## 3. Ambedkar's Ethics of Social Justice

B.R. Ambedkar developed an ethics rooted in the experience of caste oppression:

| Principle | Content |
|-----------|---------|
| **Liberty, Equality, Fraternity** | Core values; borrowed from French Revolution but deepened by Buddhist thought |
| **Anti-caste** | Caste is the antithesis of morality; it assigns dignity based on birth, not character |
| **Buddhist ethics** | Converted to Buddhism in 1956; found in Buddhist ethics (Panchshila, Ashtangikmarg) a non-hierarchical spiritual framework |
| **Constitutional morality** | Democracy requires a moral culture — respect for rules and others even when powerful |

## 4. Emotional Intelligence (EI)

**Daniel Goleman** (*Emotional Intelligence*, 1995) popularized EI:

**Four Domains of EI (Goleman's model):**

| Domain | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Self-awareness** | Knowing your own emotions, strengths, weaknesses |
| **Self-management** | Controlling impulses; adaptability; managing difficult emotions |
| **Social awareness** | Empathy; sensing others' emotions; organizational awareness |
| **Relationship management** | Inspiring, influencing, developing others; managing conflict |

**EQ vs IQ in Leadership:**
- Research shows EQ contributes more than IQ to leadership effectiveness at senior levels
- In civil services: a DM without empathy cannot handle disaster relief; a negotiator without social awareness cannot resolve conflict

## 5. Attitude — Formation and Change

**Attitude**: A learned predisposition to respond in a consistently favorable or unfavorable way to a person, object, or idea.

### ABC Model of Attitude

| Component | Description | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| **Affective** (Feeling) | Emotional response | "I feel disgusted by corruption" |
| **Behavioral** | Action tendency | "I will file RTI against corrupt official" |
| **Cognitive** | Belief/knowledge | "Corruption is economically harmful" |

### Attitude Formation

- **Direct experience**: Personal encounters shape attitudes (Dalit who faced discrimination)
- **Social learning**: Observing family, peers, media — children adopt parental attitudes
- **Classical conditioning**: Associating an object with pleasure/pain
- **Operant conditioning**: Rewarded behaviors → positive attitudes

### Attitude Change (Persuasion)

**Festinger's Cognitive Dissonance Theory:** People experience discomfort when holding inconsistent beliefs → they change attitude to reduce dissonance.

**Factors affecting persuasion:**
- **Source credibility**: We change attitude when source is trustworthy and expert
- **Message quality**: Strong arguments change attitude of engaged audience
- **Receiver involvement**: High personal relevance → careful processing → lasting change

## Quick Revision — High-Frequency APPSC Facts

**Consequentialism:**
- Bentham (1789): Greatest happiness for greatest number; felicific calculus; quantitative
- Mill (1863): Higher vs. lower pleasures; qualitative utilitarianism

**Deontology:**
- Kant: **Categorical Imperative** — "act only on principles you could universalize"
- Three formulations: Universal Law, Humanity Formula (never use as mere means), Kingdom of Ends
- **Good will** = only unconditionally good thing; duty for duty's sake
- W.D. Ross: Prima facie duties — multiple, can conflict

**Virtue Ethics:**
- Aristotle: Eudaimonia; **Golden Mean** (virtue = mean between extremes); habit builds virtue

**Gandhian Ethics:**
- Ahimsa + Satyagraha + Sarvodaya + **Trusteeship** + **Antyodaya** (last person first)
- Means = end in the making (opposed to "ends justify means")
- Antyodaya ≠ Sarvodaya: Antyodaya = prioritize the last; Sarvodaya = welfare of all

**Ambedkar:** Liberty, Equality, Fraternity; anti-caste; Buddhist ethics (1956 conversion); constitutional morality

**EI (Goleman):** 4 domains — Self-awareness, Self-management, Social awareness, Relationship management

**Attitude:** ABC (Affective, Behavioral, Cognitive); Cognitive Dissonance (Festinger)
