## The Big Picture

The Indian economy is a paradox: a country that is the world's 5th largest by GDP (nominal) yet ranks 134th on the Human Development Index. It has the fastest-growing major economy in the world yet 90% of its workforce is in the informal sector. Its agriculture employs nearly half the population but contributes only 17% of GDP.

For APPSC candidates, economic concepts appear in every paper. The key is connecting the concepts to India's policy reality: GDP measurement → Planning Commission → Five Year Plans → LPG Reforms → NITI Aayog → current policy. The theoretical concepts (GDP, GNP, NNP) anchor the policy narrative.

## 1. National Income — The Vocabulary of Economic Measurement

Every economics question in APPSC ultimately reduces to these five measures. Know the chain:

| Term | Formula | What It Measures |
|---|---|---|
| **GDP** (Gross Domestic Product) | Total value of goods & services produced **within India's borders** in a year | Production by all producers on Indian soil (including foreigners) |
| **GNP** (Gross National Product) | GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA) | Production by **Indian nationals** anywhere in the world |
| **NNP** (Net National Product) | GNP − Depreciation | GNP after accounting for wear and tear on capital goods |
| **NNP at Factor Cost** = **National Income** | NNP at Market Price − (Indirect Taxes − Subsidies) | Income actually earned by factors of production |
| **Per Capita Income** | National Income ÷ Population | Average standard of living indicator |

> 📌 **Exam Trap:** GDP measures production **within borders** — a Japanese factory in India adds to India's GDP. GNP measures production by **Indian nationals** — an Indian IT worker in the USA adds to India's GNP. The difference is Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA = income earned abroad − income paid to foreigners in India).

**GDP at Current vs. Constant Prices:**
- **Nominal GDP (current prices)**: Uses today's prices — includes inflation effects
- **Real GDP (constant prices)**: Uses base year (2011–12) prices — measures actual output growth
- **GDP Deflator** = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100 — measures average price level change

**India's GDP Base Year**: Currently **2011–12** (revised from 2004–05 in 2015).

## 2. Methods of Measuring National Income

Three equivalent approaches (all should give the same result):

1. **Product/Output Method**: Sum of value added at each stage of production. **Avoids double-counting** by using only value-added at each stage.
2. **Income Method**: Sum of all factor incomes paid — wages (labor), rent (land), interest (capital), profit (enterprise).
3. **Expenditure Method**: GDP = **C + I + G + (X − M)**
   - C = Private Consumption, I = Investment, G = Government Expenditure, X − M = Net Exports (Trade Balance)

## 3. Economic Planning — From Five Year Plans to NITI Aayog

India adopted economic planning at independence, inspired by the Soviet model. The Planning Commission (1950) directed investment, set targets, and allocated funds to states. But the system became rigid over time — hence its replacement.

| Feature | Planning Commission (1950–2014) | NITI Aayog (2015–present) |
|---|---|---|
| **Established** | 1950 (executive resolution) | January 2015 |
| **Nature** | Allocative — distributed funds to states | Advisory — think tank, no fund allocation |
| **States' Role** | Passive recipients | Active participants (Governing Council with CMs) |
| **Approach** | Top-down central planning | Bottom-up, cooperative federalism |
| **Headed by** | Prime Minister (ex-officio) | Prime Minister (ex-officio) + CEO |

### Key Five Year Plans

| Plan | Period | Focus | Key Achievement |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1st** | 1951–56 | Agriculture, Damodar Valley | Exceeded targets; Harrod-Domar model |
| **2nd** | 1956–61 | Heavy industry | Bhilai, Durgapur, Rourkela steel plants; Nehru-Mahalanobis model |
| **4th** | 1969–74 | "Remove Poverty" (Garibi Hatao) | Bank nationalization (1969), Green Revolution gains |
| **8th** | 1992–97 | LPG reforms integration | First plan post-liberalization |
| **12th** | 2012–17 | Inclusive and sustainable growth | Last formal Five Year Plan |

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** The **2nd Five Year Plan** (Nehru-Mahalanobis model) is the most-tested plan. It prioritized **heavy industry** (steel, capital goods) over consumer goods and agriculture — creating the PSU-dominated "commanding heights" economy. This worked for infrastructure but created an inefficient manufacturing sector and left agriculture underdeveloped. Understanding this explains why 1991 reforms were necessary.

**Post-Plan Era (after 12th Plan):**
- NITI Aayog replaced Planning Commission in 2015
- Three-Year Action Agenda (2017–20) → National Development Agenda 2022 → India@100 Strategy 2047
- No formal Five Year Plans

## 4. Sectors of the Indian Economy

| Sector | Share of GDP | Share of Employment | Key Industries |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary** (Agriculture, Mining, Forestry, Fishing) | ~17% | ~45% | Crops, livestock, fishing, minerals |
| **Secondary** (Manufacturing, Construction, Utilities) | ~28% | ~25% | Steel, textiles, cement, IT hardware |
| **Tertiary** (Services) | ~55% | ~30% | IT, banking, retail, education, health |

> 📌 **Exam Note:** The **structural paradox** — agriculture employs 45% of people but contributes only 17% of GDP — indicates very low agricultural productivity. The inverse is true for services (contributes 55% but employs 30%). Bridging this gap through agricultural transformation is India's central development challenge.

## 5. Monetary Policy — How RBI Manages Money Supply

**Monetary Policy Committee (MPC):**
- Established under the **RBI Act, 1934** (amended 2016)
- 6 members: 3 from RBI (Governor chairs) + 3 external members appointed by Central Government
- Meets **bi-monthly** (every 2 months) — 6 times a year
- **Inflation Target**: Keep **CPI Inflation at 4%** (±2% tolerance = range of 2%–6%)
- If inflation stays above 6% for 3 consecutive quarters, MPC must explain to Parliament

**Key Policy Rates (approximate; subject to change):**

| Rate | Role |
|---|---|
| **Repo Rate** (~6.25%) | Rate at which RBI lends to commercial banks for short term; **raising repo = tighter money = lower inflation** |
| **Reverse Repo Rate** (Repo − 0.25%) | Rate at which RBI borrows from commercial banks; absorbs liquidity |
| **CRR** (Cash Reserve Ratio, ~4%) | % of deposits banks MUST keep as cash with RBI; no interest paid; directly reduces credit creation |
| **SLR** (Statutory Liquidity Ratio, ~18%) | % of deposits banks must hold in liquid assets (gold, government securities); indirect monetary control |
| **MSF** (Marginal Standing Facility, Repo + 0.25%) | Emergency overnight borrowing window for banks |

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** Raising the **Repo Rate** makes borrowing more expensive → less credit in the economy → less spending → inflation falls. This is the MPC's primary tool. Raising CRR reduces the money multiplier directly — it forces banks to park money with RBI rather than lend it.

## 6. Fiscal Policy — Government Revenue and Expenditure

**Fiscal policy** is managed by the **Ministry of Finance** through the Union Budget (presented on **1 February** annually).

**Key Deficit Concepts:**
| Deficit | Formula | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue Deficit** | Revenue Expenditure − Revenue Receipts | Govt. spends more on day-to-day operations than it earns; borrowed money goes to consumption |
| **Fiscal Deficit** | Total Expenditure − Total Receipts (excl. borrowings) | Total borrowing requirement; most-used indicator |
| **Primary Deficit** | Fiscal Deficit − Interest Payments | Current borrowing need excluding past debt servicing; shows if new policy is adding debt |

**FRBM Act (Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act), 2003:**
- Mandates fiscal discipline; target: Fiscal Deficit = **3% of GDP**
- N.K. Singh Committee (2017): Recommended debt-to-GDP ratio target of **60%** (Centre 40% + States 20%)

**Types of Taxes:**
| Direct Taxes | Indirect Taxes |
|---|---|
| Paid directly by the person (Income Tax, Corporate Tax) | Collected by intermediary and passed to government (GST, Customs) |
| Progressive — higher income pays more % | Regressive — same % on everyone, hits poor harder proportionally |
| Administered by **CBDT** | Administered by **CBIC** |

**GST (Goods & Services Tax):**
- Implemented: **1 July 2017**
- Dual structure: **CGST** (Central) + **SGST** (State) on intra-state; **IGST** on inter-state
- **101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016** — added Article 279A creating the GST Council

## 7. Poverty & Unemployment

### Poverty Line Committees

| Committee | Year | Basis | Rural (₹/month) | Urban (₹/month) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lakdawala** | 1993 | Calorie norms | — | — |
| **Tendulkar** | 2009 | Monthly Per Capita Consumption Expenditure (MPCE) | **816** | **1,000** |
| **Rangarajan** | 2014 | Revised MPCE | **972** | **1,407** |

> 📌 **Exam Trap:** India's poverty line is based on **consumption expenditure**, NOT income. The Tendulkar Committee figures (2011–12 prices) are the most commonly cited.

**Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI):**
- Published jointly by **UNDP and OPHI** (Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative)
- Captures deprivation across 3 dimensions and 10 indicators (health, education, living standards)
- India: reduced MPI poverty from 55% (2005–06) to ~15% (2022–23)

### Types of Unemployment

| Type | Cause | Where Common |
|---|---|---|
| **Structural** | Skills mismatch — economy changes but workers don't adapt | Post-industrial transitions |
| **Cyclical** | Economic downturns reduce demand for labor | Recessions |
| **Frictional** | Temporary gap between jobs | All economies |
| **Seasonal** | Work available only part of the year | Agriculture, tourism |
| **Disguised** | Workers appear employed but add near-zero output | Indian agriculture — key APPSC concept |

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** **Disguised unemployment** is the most APPSC-relevant type. In a small farm, if 5 family members work but only 2 are needed for the output, the other 3 are "disguised unemployed" — they appear employed but could be removed without reducing output. This explains why India's farm productivity is low and why rural-to-urban migration doesn't hurt agricultural output.

- CRR reduces credit directly; SLR reduces credit indirectly
