# Administrative Theory & Concepts

> *Frederick Taylor timed workers with a stopwatch. Elton Mayo talked to them. Both were right — efficiency matters, but so does the human being doing the work. Every theory of administration since has been trying to reconcile these two truths.*

**Covers:** G1 Mains Paper 3 (Public Administration) | G2 Paper 3 (Governance)

## The Big Picture

Public Administration as a discipline studies how governments implement policy and deliver services. The theories of administration evolved in three waves: Classical theories (1880s–1930s) focused on efficiency and structure; Human Relations theories (1930s–1950s) discovered that people are not machines; and Modern approaches (1950s–present) recognized that administration is political, systems-based, and value-laden.

For APPSC, the key knowledge areas are: Classical theory thinkers and their contributions, the Human Relations school (Mayo's Hawthorne experiments), Weber's bureaucracy, New Public Management, and Good Governance as defined by UNDP. This topic tests conceptual understanding — not just names but what each theory claimed and why it was challenged.

## 1. Classical Theories of Administration

### Frederick Taylor — Scientific Management (1911)

Frederick Taylor's *Principles of Scientific Management* (1911) was the first systematic theory of organizational efficiency:

| Principle | Content |
|-----------|---------|
| **One best way** | Every task has one most efficient method — find it through systematic study (time-and-motion studies) |
| **Scientific selection** | Match worker to task scientifically, not randomly |
| **Piece-rate wage** | Pay workers per unit produced — incentivize performance |
| **Separation of thinking and doing** | Management plans; workers execute — sharp division |

**Criticisms of Taylor:**
- Treats workers as mechanical extensions of machinery
- Ignores social and psychological needs
- Led to speedup and worker resentment
- Assumed workers are only motivated by money (later proved wrong)

### Henri Fayol — Administrative Management (1916)

While Taylor focused on the shop floor, Fayol (French mining executive) focused on **top management**. His *General and Industrial Management* (1916) identified:

**14 Principles of Management** (key ones):
1. Division of Work
2. Authority and Responsibility
3. Unity of Command — each worker should have ONE boss
4. Unity of Direction — one plan for each group of activities
5. Scalar Chain — hierarchy from top to bottom
6. Esprit de Corps — team spirit

**Functions of Management (Fayol's 5):** Planning, Organizing, Commanding, Coordinating, Controlling

### Luther Gulick — POSDCORB (1937)

Luther Gulick defined the functions of the chief executive using the acronym **POSDCORB**:

| Letter | Function |
|--------|---------|
| **P** | Planning |
| **O** | Organizing |
| **S** | Staffing |
| **D** | Directing |
| **Co** | Coordinating |
| **R** | Reporting |
| **B** | Budgeting |

> 📌 **Exam Note:** **POSDCORB** is one of the most-tested memory items in Public Administration. Gulick used this to define what top administrators actually do. Note that it has **7 elements** (P-O-S-D-Co-R-B), and **"Co" = Coordinating** (not a single letter). The "Co" is often tested.

### Max Weber — Bureaucracy Theory

Max Weber (German sociologist) described the **ideal type of bureaucracy** as the most rational form of organization:

**Characteristics of Weber's Ideal Bureaucracy:**

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Hierarchy** | Clear chain of command; each official answers to one superior |
| **Rules and regulations** | Fixed, written rules govern all decisions — no personal discretion |
| **Impersonality** | Officials apply rules uniformly; no personal favoritism |
| **Division of labor** | Each official has a specialized function |
| **Meritocracy** | Appointment by qualification and examination, not inheritance |
| **Written records** | All official acts documented in files |
| **Full-time career** | Officials serve full-time; separate personal and official property |

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** Weber's bureaucracy is an **ideal type** — a theoretical model, not a description of any actual government. Real bureaucracies fall short in various ways. Weber himself acknowledged that bureaucracy has a "steel cage" quality — once established, it becomes self-perpetuating and can resist political control. This is why every government that creates a bureaucracy then spends decades trying to reform it.

**Weber's Three Types of Authority:**
1. **Traditional authority** — based on custom and tradition (kings, hereditary chiefs)
2. **Charismatic authority** — based on personal qualities (revolutionary leaders, prophets)
3. **Legal-rational authority** — based on rules and law → THIS produces bureaucracy

## 2. Human Relations School

### Elton Mayo — Hawthorne Experiments (1927–1932)

**Elton Mayo** (Australian psychologist working at Harvard) conducted studies at the Hawthorne plant of Western Electric Company (Chicago) from 1927 to 1932. These experiments revolutionized management theory.

**The experiments and their findings:**

| Experiment | What Was Tested | What Was Found |
|-----------|----------------|----------------|
| Illumination experiment | Does better lighting improve productivity? | Productivity improved regardless of lighting level — even when lighting was reduced |
| Relay Assembly Test Room | Impact of rest breaks, shorter hours, incentives | Productivity kept rising even when improvements were withdrawn |
| Bank Wiring Observation Room | Natural work group behavior | Workers enforced unofficial group norms — deliberately limited output ("soldiering") |
| Interviews | Why do workers behave as they do? | Workers have complex emotional needs; need to feel heard |

**Mayo's Conclusions (Human Relations Theory):**
1. **Social factors** determine productivity more than physical conditions
2. Workers are **not isolated individuals** — they are members of social groups
3. **Informal groups** have powerful norms that override management rules
4. Workers need **recognition, belonging, and participation** — not just wages
5. Management must consider the **whole person**, not just the task

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** The **Hawthorne Effect** is the finding that workers perform better simply because they know they are being observed — regardless of actual conditions. This was the accidental discovery that launched Human Relations theory. The implication: attention from management improves morale and productivity even when no material changes are made.

### Mary Parker Follett — Integration Theory

Mary Parker Follett (1920s) contributed ideas before Mayo but is grouped with Human Relations:
- **"Power with" not "power over"**: Leaders should integrate, not dominate
- **Constructive conflict**: Conflict can be creative if integrated rather than suppressed
- Regarded as a pioneer of **participatory management** and modern organizational theory

## 3. Modern Approaches

### Simon's Decision-Making Theory

**Herbert Simon** (*Administrative Behaviour*, 1947) challenged Classical theory's assumption of "economic man":
- Real administrators do not maximize — they **satisfice** (find a satisfactory, not optimal, solution)
- **Bounded rationality**: Human decision-making is limited by time, information, and cognitive capacity
- Administration is fundamentally about **making decisions under uncertainty**

### Systems Approach

Views organizations as open systems interacting with their environment:
- **Inputs** → **Process** → **Outputs** → **Feedback** → back to inputs
- No organization exists in isolation — all are influenced by their environment
- Key thinker: **Chester Barnard** (also contributed the idea of "zone of acceptance" — workers accept authority within limits)

### Contingency Theory

There is **no one best way** to manage — the best structure depends on the situation:
- Size of organization, technology, environment, strategy all affect what structure works
- Rejects the universal prescriptions of Classical theory
- "It depends" — the honest answer to most administrative questions

## 4. New Public Management (NPM)

**New Public Management** emerged in the 1980s (Reagan–Thatcher era) as a critique of traditional bureaucracy:

| Traditional Public Administration | New Public Management |
|-----------------------------------|----------------------|
| Rule-bound processes | Results-oriented outcomes |
| Monopoly government delivery | Market mechanisms, privatization, contracting out |
| Citizens as subjects | Citizens as customers |
| Hierarchical control | Managerial autonomy and accountability |
| Input/process focus | Performance measurement, KPIs |
| Job security, seniority | Performance-related pay, contracts |

**NPM in India:**
- Reforms since 1991 liberalization
- **Citizen's Charter** (1997) — service delivery commitments
- E-governance (digital delivery of services)
- PPP models in infrastructure
- Performance budgeting (outcome budgeting)
- RFD (Results Framework Document) for ministries

> 📌 **Exam Note:** **New Public Management is NOT about making government small** — it is about making government efficient. The slogan is "steering rather than rowing" (Osborne & Gaebler, *Reinventing Government*, 1992). Government should set direction (policy) and let others implement (market, NGOs, PPP). Pure privatization goes beyond NPM.

## 5. Good Governance

**UNDP's 8 Characteristics of Good Governance** (1997, widely used):

| Characteristic | Meaning |
|----------------|---------|
| **Participatory** | Citizens participate in decision-making |
| **Rule of Law** | Legal framework fairly enforced; independent judiciary |
| **Transparent** | Decisions and implementation are open to scrutiny |
| **Responsive** | Institutions serve all stakeholders within reasonable timeframe |
| **Consensus-oriented** | Mediation between different interests; broad consensus on what is best |
| **Equitable and Inclusive** | All members of society feel included; especially vulnerable groups |
| **Effective and Efficient** | Processes and institutions produce results meeting needs; best use of resources |
| **Accountable** | Government institutions, private sector, and civil society are accountable to the public |

> 📌 **Exam Note:** The UNDP gives **8 characteristics** of Good Governance. In MCQs, options often include 6, 7, 8, or 9 — the answer is **8**. The most commonly tested individual characteristic is **"Accountable"** and **"Participatory"**. Also remember: Good Governance is NOT just about government — it includes **private sector** and **civil society** accountability.
