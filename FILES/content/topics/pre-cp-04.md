# LPG Reforms & Regulatory Bodies

> *1991 was India's economic inflection point — the moment India moved from a license-permit raj to a market economy. Everything in Indian economic policy since then traces back to that summer.*

**Covers:** G1 Prelims Unit 4 | G2 Paper 1 Section B | G1 Mains Paper 3
**Sources:** NCERT Class 11 & 12 Economics, M. Laxmikanth, Economic Survey of India

---

## The Big Picture

In June 1991, India faced bankruptcy. Foreign exchange reserves had fallen to $1.2 billion — barely 3 weeks of import cover. The Indian government pledged 67 tonnes of gold to the Bank of England to obtain a loan from the IMF. In exchange, India committed to structural reforms: opening up the economy, reducing the role of government, and integrating with global markets.

What followed was the **New Economic Policy (NEP) of 1991** — the LPG reforms. The three pillars were Liberalization (removing controls), Privatization (reducing state ownership), and Globalization (opening to world trade and investment). The Finance Minister who engineered the reforms was **Dr. Manmohan Singh**, under PM **P.V. Narasimha Rao**.

These reforms transformed India — but unevenly. Services (especially IT) exploded. Manufacturing grew moderately. Agriculture was largely left behind. Understanding this transformation is essential for APPSC Mains answers on economic policy.

## 1. LPG Reforms (1991) — Content and Impact

### Liberalization

**Definition**: Reducing government controls, regulations, and restrictions on economic activity.

**Key Measures:**
- Abolition of the **Industrial Licensing System** (License Raj) for most industries
- Removal of **MRTP Act restrictions** on monopolies and large businesses (MRTP Act 1969 → replaced by Competition Act 2002)
- Deregulation of interest rates
- Reduction of import tariffs and removal of quantitative restrictions on imports
- Foreign Investment allowed in most sectors (FDI and FII)

### Privatization

**Definition**: Transfer of ownership or management of public sector enterprises (PSUs) to private entities.

**Key Forms:**
- **Full Privatization**: Complete transfer of ownership
- **Disinvestment**: Government sells a portion of its stake in PSUs (while retaining majority)
- **Strategic Disinvestment**: Government sells majority stake including management control

**Examples in India:**
- Air India → sold to Tata Group (2022)
- BALCO, HPCL, IBP → various stages of disinvestment
- **Navaratna/Maharatna status**: Gives profitable PSUs more autonomy in investment decisions

### Globalization

**Definition**: Integrating the national economy with the global economy through trade, investment flows, and technology transfer.

**Key Measures:**
- Reduction of customs duties (from 150%+ average in 1991 to <15% today)
- Convertibility of Rupee on current account (for trade transactions)
- FDI liberalization — automatic route for most sectors
- India joining WTO in 1995

### Impact Assessment

| Sector | Impact |
|---|---|
| **IT and Services** | Explosive growth; India became the world's back-office; Bengaluru, Hyderabad as global IT hubs |
| **Manufacturing** | Moderate growth; labor law complexity limited competitiveness |
| **Agriculture** | Largely bypassed; input subsidies reduced; terms of trade turned against farmers |
| **Poverty** | Declined significantly (from ~45% in 1994 to ~20% by 2010); but inequality increased |
| **Foreign Exchange** | From $1.2 billion (1991) to $600+ billion (2024) |

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** The LPG reforms created "**jobless growth**" in the formal sector — GDP grew at 7–8% per year, but formal employment creation lagged. The IT sector employs ~5 million directly (out of 500 million workforce). The challenge of converting economic growth into employment remains central to Indian economic policy today.

## 2. Major Regulatory Bodies

Post-liberalization, new sectors emerged that needed independent regulation separate from the government. Regulatory bodies were created as quasi-independent statutory authorities.

### SEBI — Securities and Exchange Board of India

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Established** | 1988 (as non-statutory advisory body) |
| **Statutory Status** | **1992** via SEBI Act 1992 — this is the key date |
| **Headquarters** | Mumbai |
| **Function** | Regulates securities markets (stock exchanges, brokers, mutual funds, FIIs); protects investors; promotes market development |

> 📌 **Exam Trap:** SEBI existed since 1988, but it was **only an advisory body** then. It became a **statutory regulatory authority** only in **1992**. Examiners state it was statutory since 1988 — that is WRONG.

### IRDAI — Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Established** | **1999** (based on Malhotra Committee recommendations 1993) |
| **Headquarters** | Hyderabad (AP — an important AP angle) |
| **Function** | Regulates and develops the insurance industry; protects policyholders; promotes fair competition |

> 📌 **Exam Note:** IRDAI is headquartered in **Hyderabad** — an AP-relevant fact. The Malhotra Committee (1993) recommended opening the insurance sector to private and foreign players after decades of nationalized monopoly (LIC for life insurance, GIC for general insurance).

### PFRDA — Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Established** | 2003 (interim), **Statutory status** from **2013** via PFRDA Act 2013 |
| **Function** | Regulates National Pension System (NPS) and pension funds; protects subscriber interests |

### CCI — Competition Commission of India

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Established** | 2009 (under Competition Act, **2002**) |
| **Function** | Prevents anti-competitive practices; eliminates cartels and abuse of dominant position; protects consumers |
| **Replaced** | MRTP Commission (under Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, 1969) |

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** The shift from MRTP Act to Competition Act represents a fundamental change in philosophy. The MRTP Act targeted the **size** of companies (big = potentially bad). The Competition Act targets **behavior** — a company can be large, but cannot abuse its market position. This is the modern antitrust standard.

## 3. Key Economic Committees

| Committee | Year | Sector | Key Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Narasimham Committee I** | 1991 | Banking | Deregulation of interest rates; reduce CRR & SLR; 4-tier banking structure |
| **Narasimham Committee II** | 1998 | Banking | Mergers of weak banks; reduction of NPAs; capital adequacy norms |
| **Vijay Kelkar Committee** | 2002 | Tax Reforms | Direct and indirect tax reforms; strengthening PPP model; rationalization of exemptions |
| **Raja Chelliah Committee** | 1991–93 | Tax Reforms | Lowering tax rates; broadening tax base; introduction of VAT |
| **Rangarajan Committee** | Multiple | Various | Poverty estimation; disinvestment policy; currency management |

> 📌 **Exam Note:** **Vijay Kelkar Committee** is frequently asked in the context of both **GST** (he first recommended a comprehensive Goods and Services Tax in India in 2002) and **infrastructure/PPP** policies. Kelkar = Tax + PPP in APPSC shorthand.

## 4. Finance Commission (Article 280)

The Finance Commission is the constitutional body that determines how Central tax revenue is divided between the Centre and States:

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Constitutional Basis** | **Article 280** |
| **Nature** | Constitutional body (not statutory, not permanent — constituted every 5 years) |
| **Appointment** | By the **President of India** |
| **Composition** | Chairman + 4 other members |
| **Core Function** | Recommends distribution of net tax proceeds between Union and States (**Vertical Devolution**) and among States (**Horizontal Devolution**) |

**15th Finance Commission (2020–2026):**
- Chaired by **N.K. Singh**
- Recommended **41%** share for states (down from 42% in 14th FC, due to creation of J&K and Ladakh as UTs — which now receive central funding separately)

> 🔑 **Key Insight:** The Finance Commission is the primary mechanism for **fiscal federalism** in India. States depend heavily on their share of Central taxes (devolution) for their budgets. The political tension between Centre and States over devolution percentages is a recurring Mains topic — larger states vs. smaller states, donor states vs. recipient states.
