Q1. According to the AP Land Reforms (Ceiling on Agricultural Holdings) Act of 1973, what is the 'Family Unit' typically defined as for calculating ceiling limits?
(a) An individual adult, regardless of marital status
(b) Husband, wife, and their unmarried minor children up to 5 members
(c) The entire joint family including grandparents and married siblings
(d) Husband, wife, and all unmarried children regardless of age

Answer: (b)
Explanation: The 1973 Act plugged loopholes from the 1961 Act by defining the 'Family Unit' as consisting of husband, wife, and minor children, up to a maximum of 5 members. For families larger than 5, an additional increment was allowed up to a maximum limit.

Q4. Why were the various Land Ceiling Acts passed by the respective state legislatures, including Andhra Pradesh, placed in the Ninth Schedule of the Indian Constitution?

(c) To protect them from being challenged in judicial courts on the grounds of violating Fundamental Rights.
(d) To automatically transfer the subject of land from the State List to the Concurrent List.

Answer: (c)
Explanation: placing land reform legislations in the Ninth Schedule protected them from judicial review under Article 31B, specifically protecting them from being struck down for violating Fundamental Rights such as the Right to Property (Article 31, before its repeal).

Q5. Which of the following is NOT a prominent reason for the failure or limited success of the 1961 AP Ceiling on Agricultural Holdings Act?
(a) The unit of application for the ceiling was the individual rather than the family.
(b) The ceiling limits set were excessively high, leaving very little surplus land.
(c) The Act provided numerous exemptions for orchards, specialized farms, and trusts.
(d) The Act strictly prohibited benami transactions and confiscated all such lands immediately.

Answer: (d)
Explanation: The Act failed precisely because benami transfers (transferring land to fictitious names or distant relatives) were rampant, and the law did not effectively curb or strictly confiscate them. Statements (a), (b), and (c) represent genuine loopholes that caused the 1961 Act to fail.

Q7. In the context of agrarian reforms in Andhra Pradesh, what was the primary purpose of the '1/70 Act' (AP Scheduled Areas Land Transfer Regulation Amendment, 1970)?
(a) To ensure that one-seventieth (1/70) of all state revenue is allocated to tribal welfare.
(b) To establish maximum ceiling limits on tribal agricultural holdings.
(c) To prohibit and nullify the transfer of land in Scheduled Areas from tribals to non-tribals.
(d) To regularize encroached forest lands for tribal communities.

Answer: (c)
Explanation: The 1/70 Act strictly prohibits the transfer of land in the Scheduled Areas to non-tribals. Any transfer, whether from a tribal or a non-tribal situated in these areas, can only be made to a tribal. This protects Adivasis from land alienation.

Q9. While evaluating the multi-dimensional impact of Land Reforms in India, comparing the success of intermediary abolition with land ceilings yields which of the following definitive conclusions?
(a) Both intermediary abolition and land ceilings were equally successful and met their redistributive targets.
(b) Intermediary abolition was largely successful, while land ceilings had limited success due to vast legal loopholes and bureaucratic delays.
(c) Land ceilings successfully redistributed wealth to the landless Dalits, but intermediary abolition completely failed to confer rights to occupancy tenants.
(d) Both programs failed because agriculture was moved from the State List to the Union List shortly after independence.

Answer: (b)
Explanation: Economic surveys and historians agree that the abolition of Zamindars (intermediaries) was the most successful aspect of land reforms. Conversely, land ceilings failed to yield significant surplus land due to benami transfers, poor land records, and political interference from landed elites.
