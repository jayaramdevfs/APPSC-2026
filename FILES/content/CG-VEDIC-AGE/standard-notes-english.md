## Unit 1: Who Were the Aryans? — Texts and Identity

Before we can study the Vedic civilization, we must understand who composed it. The term "Aryan" is linguistic, not racial. It referred to a cultural community that spoke Indo-European languages. In the Rig Veda, the term *arya* occurs thirty-six times and generally denotes a cultural community, not a race or skin colour. The idea of Aryans as a biological race was a 19th-century European invention that was then catastrophically misused by the Nazis — it has no basis in the ancient texts.

The principal sources for understanding Aryan culture are: the *Rig Veda* (assigned to roughly 1500 BCE, though later additions may date to 1000 BCE), the *Zend-Avesta* of Iran (earliest parts ~1400 BCE), and Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey* (~900–800 BCE). These texts, though composed in different regions, share a family of cognate languages and cultural traits — together they allow us to reconstruct the common heritage of the Indo-European-speaking peoples.

The cultural contents of these texts point to the late Neolithic and early Bronze Age. The climate and flora described (temperate conditions, birch trees, absence of tropical animals like the tiger and rhinoceros) suggest the speakers lived in Eastern Europe and Central Asia, not in warm tropical regions. The genetic marker M17, present in 40% of Central Asian steppe people, is found in 35% of Hindi-speaking people in Delhi — providing biological evidence for the Central Asian origin and migration of the Indo-Aryans into the subcontinent.

## Unit 2: The Central Aryan Traits

Several specific cultural traits distinguish Aryan societies across the Indo-European world:

**The Horse**: The horse was the defining animal of Aryan culture. The Sanskrit word *asva* (horse) has cognates in Avestan, Greek, Latin, and all Indo-European languages. In the Rig Veda, *asva* occurs 215 times — no other animal is mentioned so frequently. The cow (*go*) occurs 176 times and the bull (*vrsabha*) 170 times — both important, but dominated by the horse-based aristocracy. The tiger and rhinoceros, characteristic of tropical India, do not appear in the Rig Veda at all. This matters enormously: these animals were absent not because the Aryans couldn't see them, but because the Rig Veda was composed when the Aryans were primarily in the northwest (Punjab, Afghanistan), where tigers and rhinos did not live.

The earliest domesticated horse is found between the Dnieper and Volga rivers in Central/Eastern Europe. It was known in the south Ural region from the 6th millennium BCE, came to Anatolia by the 4th millennium BCE, and only came into general use across Eurasia around 2000 BCE. In South Asia, the earliest true horse is found at the **Pirak complex** in Baluchistan around 1700 BCE. Horse remains and horse furnishings appear in the **Gandhara grave culture** (Swat valley, NWFP, Pakistan) from around 1400 BCE. Horse bones are found at **Bhagwanpura** in Haryana in layers dated 1600–1000 BCE (overlapping PGW and late Harappan pottery). By contrast, no clear horse bones were found in the most recent excavations at Harappa (1986–95), and none at Dholavira (Gujarat) in extensive excavations. This timing — horse appearing in South Asia ~1700 BCE and becoming important after 1400 BCE — is fully consistent with the Rig Veda being composed around 1500 BCE.

**The War Chariot**: Horse-drawn chariots with spoked wheels were an Aryan military technology. The *vajapeya* chariot race is mentioned in later Vedic texts and was also a Greek practice (described by Homer). The wheeled chariot originated in western Asia around the 4th millennium BCE. Spoked wheels (lighter and faster than solid wheels) appear at Hissar (Iran) and the north Caucasus around 2300 BCE, and by 1500 BCE are found across eastern Europe, western Asia, and Central Asia. Critically, **spoked wheels are NOT found at Harappa or Mohenjo-daro** — all toy carts found there have solid wheels. This is another sharp contrast between Harappan and Aryan material cultures.

**Cremation**: The Harappans practised earth burial (inhumation). Cremation appears in the later Harappan phase at Cemetery-H sites and in Gujarat, likely as a result of contact with incoming Aryan peoples. Post-cremation burial (burnt bones deposited in urns) appears in the Swat valley from the second half of the 2nd millennium BCE — consistent with Aryan arrival.

**The Cult of Fire and Soma**: Fire (*agni*) was central to both Vedic and Avestan (Iranian) religion. The fire altar or *vedi* is mentioned in the Rig Veda. So important was fire worship that it became an Indo-Iranian cultural trait shared between Vedic Aryans and Zoroastrian Iranians. Soma was a ritual intoxicating drink used in both Vedic and Avestan ceremonies (called *haoma* in Avestan). The soma plant has been identified as *ephedra*, small twigs of which have been found in vessels used for drinking rituals at the temple of Togolok-21 in Margiana (south-eastern Turkmenistan) — precisely the Central Asian area from which the Aryans migrated into India.

## Unit 3: Arrival of the Aryans — The Migration Route

The Indo-Aryans migrated into the Indian subcontinent from two regions of Central Asia. The first is archaeologically known as the **Andronovo culture**, which covered almost the whole of Central Asia during the 2nd millennium BCE. The Andronovo culture manifests all the important elements of Aryan life: stock breeding, widespread use of horse, spoked wheels, cremation, pit-dwellings, and the soma drink. It is therefore regarded as proto-Indo-Iranian — the parent culture that eventually sent branches into both Iran (becoming the Avestan-speaking Iranians) and India (becoming the Vedic Aryans).

The second source area is the **Bactria–Margiana Archaeological Complex (BMAC)**, dated 1900–1500 BCE, covering Bactria (Afghanistan/Balkh), Margiana (Turkmenistan/Uzbekistan). Around 1500 BCE, the BMAC shows evidence of domestic horses, chariots with spoked wheels, partial cremation, and the svastika motif. The herdsmen from the BMAC area damaged the pre-Aryan proto-urban cultures there and then moved toward the Indian subcontinent. That is why horse remains and post-cremation burials appear in the Swat valley from about 1400 BCE.

The earliest Aryans settled in what R.S. Sharma calls "the Land of the Seven Rivers" — eastern Afghanistan, the North-West Frontier Province, Punjab, and the fringes of western UP. The rivers mentioned in the Rig Veda include the Kubha (Kabul river, Afghanistan), the Sindhu (Indus), its five tributaries, and the Sarasvati — described as the *naditama* (best of rivers). R.S. Sharma notes that the Rig Vedic Sarasvati may originally have been the Avestan Harakhwati or the present Helmand river in south Afghanistan, and that the name was transferred to the Ghaggar-Hakra river in Haryana when the Aryans moved into India.

The Aryans migrated in **several waves**, not in one single movement. The earliest wave, represented by the Rig Vedic people, came to the subcontinent around 1500 BCE. They came into conflict with the indigenous inhabitants called *dasas* and *dasyus*. The *dasas* may have been an early branch of the Aryans themselves (the term appears in Iranian literature too); the *dasyus* were more likely the original pre-Aryan inhabitants of India. The Rig Veda calls one Aryan chief Trasadasyu (the slaughter of the dasyus) — suggesting he was named for his victories against the indigenous population.

## Unit 4: The Early Vedic (Rig Vedic) Period (~1500–1000 BCE)

### Geography and Archaeological Evidence

The Rig Vedic people lived primarily in the northwestern region described above. They did NOT live in cities — the term *pur* in the Rig Veda means a dwelling unit or cluster of units, not a city or fort. They lived in fortified mud settlements.

The archaeological site of **Bhagwanpura** in Haryana, along with three other sites in Punjab, has yielded **Painted Grey Ware (PGW)** along with late Harappan pottery, dated to 1600–1000 BCE — which corresponds to the Rig Vedic period. The geographical area of these four sites also coincides with the area described in the Rig Veda. Horse bones have been found at Bhagwanpura. Iron objects are absent, suggesting this was a pre-iron phase of PGW culture coinciding with the Rig Vedic phase. An interesting find was a thirteen-roomed mud house — possibly the house of an extended family or a tribal chief.

### Material Life — Cattle and Agriculture

The Rig Vedic people were primarily **pastoral** — cattle rearers. The Rig Veda has so many references to the cow and bull that R.S. Sharma describes the Rig Vedic people as "a predominantly pastoral people." Most of their wars were fought over cows — the term for war in the Rig Veda is *gavishthi*, literally "search for cows." Gifts made to priests always consisted of cows and women slaves, never land. Land was not yet a well-established form of private property.

They also practised agriculture — the ploughshare is mentioned in the earliest part of the Rig Veda (possibly an interpolation), and they knew about sowing, harvesting, and threshing. But agriculture was secondary to cattle rearing. The term *ayas* (used for copper or bronze) shows metalworking was known. The Rig Veda mentions carpenters, chariot-makers, weavers, leather workers, and potters as artisans. However, there is no clear evidence of regular trade — the Aryans principally used land routes, and the word *samudra* in the Rig Veda means "a stretch of water," not necessarily the sea in the maritime sense.

### Tribal Organization and Polity

Rig Vedic society was organized around the **tribe** (*jana*). The term *jana* occurs about 275 times in the Rig Veda, while *janapada* (people's territory) is not used even once. People were attached to the tribe, not to territory — neither control over territory nor a kingdom was yet established. The term *vis* (clan sub-unit) is mentioned 170 times; the term *grama* referred to a smaller fighting unit of the tribe.

The chief of the tribe was called **rajan**. The rajan's post was becoming hereditary but he did not exercise unlimited power — he had to reckon with tribal assemblies. Several assemblies are mentioned: the **sabha**, **samiti**, **vidatha**, and **gana**. These exercised deliberative, military, and religious functions. Even women attended the sabha and vidatha in Rig Vedic times — a striking detail that contrasts with their exclusion in later Vedic times.

The rajan was the protector of his tribe — he protected its cattle, fought its wars, and offered prayers to the gods on its behalf. He received **bali** (voluntary offerings) from his kinsmen. The king was assisted by the **purohita** (chief priest) — the two priests most prominent in Rig Vedic times were Vasishtha (conservative) and Vishvamitra (liberal). Vishvamitra composed the *Gayatri* mantra to widen the Aryan world. The military commander was the **senani**. The **vrajapati** had authority over large stretches of pasture grounds. The **gramani** was initially just the head of a small kin-based fighting unit but gradually became the head of the village.

There was no standing army — the king mustered a militia in times of war from tribal groups called *vrata*, *gana*, *grama*, and *sardha*. There was no civil or territorial administration, since people were in a state of perpetual expansion and migration.

The most important tribal conflict was the **Battle of Ten Kings** (*Dasharajna*), fought on the river Parushni (identified with the Ravi). The Bharata clan under Chief Divodasa fought against a confederacy of ten chiefs (five Aryan, five non-Aryan). The Bharatas won. Subsequently, the Bharatas joined with the Purus to form the Kuru people. The Kurus then combined with the Panchalas. The Kuru-Panchala people established their rule in the upper Gangetic basin — this combination would become historically important in the Later Vedic period. India is named *Bharatavarsha* after the Bharata tribe.

### Social Structure

Kinship was the basis of social structure. The family unit was the *kula* (household), denoted in the Rig Veda primarily by the term *griha*. The family was a large, patriarchal, joint unit — in early Indo-European languages, a single word denoted nephew, grandson, cousin, etc., indicating that family differentiation had not yet occurred into separate nuclear households. Sons were prayed for; daughters were not mentioned with the same desire (this was a patriarchal society).

The Rig Veda displays some social differentiation — a consciousness of colour (*varna*). Indo-Aryan language speakers were fair and indigenous inhabitants dark in complexion, and colour may have initially identified social orders. The Rig Veda mentions the *arya varna* and *dasa varna*. Society was divided into three occupational groups: warriors, priests, and common people (similar to Iranian society). The **fourth division — the shudras** — appears only in the tenth book of the Rig Veda, which is the latest addition. Shudras were the conquered peoples made to serve. Slaves (*dasas/dasyus*) were primarily women, used for domestic purposes — NOT for agriculture or productive work. Overall, however, since the economy was mainly pastoral and not agricultural, social divisions were not yet sharp. No gifts of land are recorded; the tribal elements in society were strong and society was "still tribal and egalitarian."

The institution of marriage was established. Levirate (a widow marrying her dead husband's brother) and widow remarriage are mentioned. No child marriage. Marriageable age in the Rig Veda appears to have been 16–17. Five women composed hymns in the Rig Veda (the later texts mention twenty). Women could attend assemblies and offer sacrifices with their husbands.

### Religion — The Rig Vedic Gods

The Aryans personified the forces of nature and worshipped them as living beings. The Rig Veda is replete with hymns to these deities.

**Indra** held the most important position — 250 hymns are devoted to him. He is called *Purandara* (destroyer of dwelling units) and is the rain god and warlord who led the Aryan soldiers to victory against their enemies.

**Agni** held the second position — 200 hymns. The fire god who acted as an intermediary between humans and the gods. Oblations offered to Agni were supposed to be carried in the form of smoke to the sky, and thus transmitted to the gods.

**Varuna** held the third position — he personified water and was supposed to uphold the natural order.

**Soma** was the god of plants. Many hymns explain the methods of preparing the intoxicating soma drink — the plant has not been satisfactorily identified, though *ephedra* is the current leading candidate.

**Sarasvati** was an important river goddess. The **Maruts** personified the storm.

Women divinities included Aditi and Usha (goddess of dawn), but they were far less prominent than male gods in a patriarchal society.

People worshipped gods for thoroughly material reasons — not for spiritual uplift or liberation, but primarily asking for *praja* (children), *pashu* (cattle), food, wealth, and health. Prayers were offered collectively by the tribe to its special deity, and Agni and Indra were invited to partake of the tribe's sacrifices.

## Unit 5: The Later Vedic Phase (~1000–500 BCE)

### Sources and Archaeological Evidence

The later Vedic period is known from texts compiled after the Rig Veda. The **Sama Veda** set the prayers of the Rig Veda to music. The **Yajur Veda** contained hymns but also rituals to accompany their recitation. The **Atharva Veda** contained charms and spells to ward off evils and diseases — its contents throw light on beliefs of non-Aryan peoples that were absorbed into Vedic culture. The **Brahmanas** were prose texts explaining the ritualistic formulae and the social and religious meanings of rituals.

Archaeologically, the later Vedic period corresponds to the **Painted Grey Ware (PGW) culture** — nearly 700 inhabited sites found in the upper Gangetic basin. These sites were inhabited by people who used earthen bowls and dishes made of painted grey pottery (the most distinctive pottery of this period) and also used **iron weapons**. The combination of later Vedic texts and PGW iron-phase archaeology allows us to form a comprehensive picture of life in the first half of the 1st millennium BCE in western UP and adjoining areas of Punjab, Haryana, and Rajasthan.

### Geographic Expansion

During the later Vedic period, the Aryans expanded dramatically from Punjab over the whole of western UP in the Ganga-Yamuna doab. The Bharatas and Purus (already allied as the Kuru people) gradually coalesced with the Panchalas. The **Kuru-Panchala people** set up their capital at **Hastinapur** (in Meerut district) — this is the capital mentioned in the Mahabharata. Excavations at Hastinapur datable to 900–500 BCE have revealed settlements and faint beginnings of town life.

The great war described in the **Mahabharata** — the Battle of Bharata between the Kauravas and Pandavas — is supposed to have been fought around **950 BCE** on the upper doab. Both sides belonged to the Kuru clan, and as a result of the war, virtually the whole of the Kuru clan was wiped out. From tradition we learn that Hastinapur was later flooded, and the remnants of the Kuru clan moved to **Kaushambi** near Allahabad.

By around 500 BCE, the Vedic people had spread from the doab further east to **Koshala** (eastern UP) and **Videha** (north Bihar). In these areas they encountered people using copper implements and black-and-red pottery. They succeeded in the second phase of expansion because they used iron weapons and horse-drawn chariots.

### Use of Iron

Iron first appears in the Gandhara area of Pakistan around 1000 BCE — iron implements buried with dead bodies have been found there. At about the same time, iron was used in eastern Punjab, western UP, Madhya Pradesh, and Rajasthan. Excavations show that **iron weapons such as arrowheads and spearheads came to be commonly used in western UP from about 800 BCE** onwards. Iron in the later Vedic texts is called *shyama* or *krishna ayas* (black metal), as opposed to *ayas* alone (which usually meant copper/bronze in the Rig Veda).

The iron axe may have been used to clear forests in the upper Gangetic basin, enabling the Vedic people to settle and farm. However, the forests may not have been very dense (rainfall ranging only 35–65 cm). Knowledge of iron spread to eastern UP and Videha towards the end of the Vedic period — the earliest iron implements there date to the 7th century BCE.

### Agriculture

Agriculture became the chief means of subsistence in the later Vedic period, replacing the primarily pastoral economy of the Rig Vedic period. The texts speak of 6, 8, 12, and even 24 oxen yoked to the plough — likely an exaggeration. Ploughing was done with a **wooden ploughshare** (iron ploughshares were rare). The Shatapatha Brahmana speaks at length about ploughing rituals. King Janaka (Sita's father) and even Balarama (Krishna's brother, called Haladhara = wielder of the plough) are associated with the plough — showing that in those days even kings and nobles did not hesitate to take to manual labour. Eventually ploughing was assigned to lower orders and prohibited for upper varnas.

**Rice** (called *vrihi*) became an important crop alongside wheat and barley. Rice was grown in the Ganga-Yamuna doab for the first time during this period — remains found at Hastinapur relate to the 8th century BCE, and at Atranjikhera (Etah district) around the same time. Rice is recommended in Vedic rituals, but wheat only rarely.

### Political Organization — From Tribe to Kingdom

A major transformation in the later Vedic period was the shift from tribal chiefdom to territorial kingship. The Rig Vedic tribal assemblies lost importance, and royal power increased. The **vidatha** completely disappeared. The **sabha** and **samiti** continued but were now controlled by warriors and brahmanas — women were no longer permitted to sit in the sabha.

New political concepts emerged: the term **rashtra** (territory) appears for the first time, and **rajya** (sovereignty, sovereign power). Tribal identity gave way to territorial identity — initially areas were named after the tribe that first settled there, but eventually the name became the name of the territory regardless of who lived there (Panchala was first a people, then became a region).

The king performed new rituals that demonstrated and reinforced his sovereignty:
- **Rajasuya** — the royal consecration sacrifice, supposed to confer supreme power on the king
- **Ashvamedha** — the horse sacrifice, which meant unquestioned control over an area in which the royal horse ran uninterrupted for a year
- **Vajapeya** — a chariot race in which the royal chariot was made to win the race against the king's own kinsmen

Taxes and tributes became regular. The officer who collected taxes was called **sangrihitri**. In the rajasuya sacrifice, as many as 240,000 cows were reportedly given as *dakshina* (gift) to the officiating priests — testimony to the enormous concentration of wealth in later Vedic times.

### Social Organization — The Varna System Hardens

The later Vedic period saw the consolidation of the **four-varna** (caste) system. The term *arya* in later Vedic texts came to encompass all four varnas — brahman, *rajanya* (or kshatriya), vaishya, and shudra. Thus, the varna system was the creation of the Vedic Aryans.

**Brahmanas** rose dramatically in importance. Initially one of sixteen classes of priests, they gradually overshadowed all other priestly groups. The growing cult of sacrifices enormously added to their power — sacrifices required trained priests who knew the precise formulae, and the brahmanas claimed a monopoly of this knowledge. The rise of brahmanas as the most important class is described by R.S. Sharma as "a peculiar development that did not occur in Aryan societies outside India" — non-Aryan elements may have had some role in the formation of the brahman varna.

**Kshatriyas (rajanya)** were the warrior nobles. Sometimes brahmanas came into conflict with kshatriyas for supremacy; however, whenever the two upper orders had to deal with the lower orders, they put aside their differences and cooperated.

**Vaishyas** constituted the common people — farmers, cattle-breeders, and artisans. They appear to have been the **only tribute payers** in later Vedic times; brahmanas and kshatriyas lived on the tributes extracted from the vaishyas. The process of making the mass of tribesmen into tribute payers (*vis* → vaishya) was long and protracted.

**Shudras** were the worst off. The *Aitareya Brahmana* describes the shudra as "the servant of another, to be made to work at will by another, and to be beaten at will." Shudras and women were denied the **upanayana** (sacred thread ceremony) and denied the right to recite the Gayatri mantra — thereby being excluded from Vedic education.

All three upper varnas shared the right to the upanayana ceremony, which marked the beginning of Vedic education. The **gotra** system emerged in later Vedic times — *gotra* literally meant "the cow pen" or the place where cattle of the entire clan are kept, but came to signify descent from a common ancestor. **Gotra exogamy** was practised: no marriage could take place between persons of the same gotra. The **ashrama** (four stages of life) system also emerged: brahmachari (student), grihastha (householder), vanaprastha (hermit), and sannyasin (ascetic). However, only the first three were clearly defined in the later Vedic texts; the sannyasi stage was not well established.

### Religion — From Prayer to Ritual

The later Vedic period saw a dramatic shift in religion. The two outstanding Rig Vedic gods, **Indra** and **Agni**, lost their former importance. **Prajapati** (the creator) came to occupy the supreme position. **Rudra** (later Shiva), the god of animals, became important. **Vishnu** was conceived as the preserver and protector.

The dominant mode of worship shifted from prayer to **sacrifice** (*yajna*). Sacrifices became far more important than prayers and assumed both public and domestic characters. The sacrificer was called the *yajamana*. Sacrifices involved the killing of animals on a large scale — especially cattle. The guest was called *goghna* (one who was fed on cattle). A guest being fed on cattle is the origin of beef eating in Vedic culture.

The formulae and sacrifices were invented and elaborated by the brahmanas who claimed a monopoly of priestly knowledge. R.S. Sharma notes that "mercenary motives cannot be ruled out." As many as **240,000 cows** were reportedly given as *dakshina* in the rajasuya sacrifice. Some priests even claimed portions of territory as *dakshina*, though the grant of land as a sacrificial fee is not established in the later Vedic period.

Signs of **idolatry** (idol worship) begin to appear in later Vedic times. Some objects began to be worshipped as symbols of divinity.

The upper doab (land of the Kuru-Panchalas) emerged as the **cradle of Aryan culture** under brahmanical influence — all the Vedic literature seems to have been compiled in this area.

26. Vidatha disappeared in later Vedic period; sabha/samiti continued but women excluded
27. Three royal rituals: rajasuya (consecration), ashvamedha (horse = territorial sovereignty), vajapeya (chariot race)
28. Gotra = clan lineage; gotra exogamy = cannot marry within same gotra
29. Upanayana (sacred thread) = denied to shudras and women in later Vedic period → beginning of formal discrimination
30. Prajapati replaced Indra as supreme deity in later Vedic pantheon
31. Andronovo culture (Central Asia, 2nd millennium BCE) = proto-Indo-Iranian; all Aryan traits present
32. BMAC (Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex) = 1900–1500 BCE; route of Aryan entry into India
33. Bhagwanpura (Haryana) = PGW + late Harappan pottery together; 1600–1000 BCE; horse bones found; 13-roomed mud house
