## Unit 1: The Geological and Evolutionary Background

Before studying the Stone Age, it is important to understand the geological backdrop in which early human life developed. The earth is over 4,600 million years old. The history of the earth is divided into four broad geological stages, of which the fourth is called the **Quaternary period**. This Quaternary period is itself divided into two epochs: the **Pleistocene** and the **Holocene**.

The Pleistocene epoch, popularly known as the Ice Age, lasted from about 2 million BC to 12,000 BC. During this long period, successive waves of intense cold periodically gripped the earth. This is significant because the entire Palaeolithic (Old Stone Age) culture of humanity developed within the Pleistocene epoch — in other words, Stone Age man lived entirely through the Ice Age. When the ice age ended around 12,000 BC, the Holocene or post-ice age epoch began, and it continues to this day. The transition from Pleistocene to Holocene brought about a dramatic warming of the climate, which in turn caused major changes in vegetation and animal life. This climatic shift is the single most important environmental factor that pushed human cultures from the Palaeolithic into the Mesolithic and eventually the Neolithic phase.

## Unit 2: Human Evolution — From Ape to Modern Man

The story of human evolution on earth is a story of gradual biological and intellectual development. Understanding this sequence is essential for understanding the tools and cultures of prehistoric India.

The earliest ancestor on the human evolutionary line was a creature called **Australopithecus**, which means "southern ape" in Latin. This species appeared on earth roughly between 5.5 million and 1.5 million years ago in southern and eastern Africa. Although it was bipedal — meaning it walked on two legs — it had a very small braincase of only about 400 cubic centimetres, which is not much larger than that of an ape. It is considered proto-human, meaning it was on the threshold of becoming human but had not fully crossed that threshold.

The first true human was **Homo habilis** ("handy" or "skilful man"), found in eastern and southern Africa about 2 to 1.5 million years ago. Homo habilis broke stone into pieces and sharpened them into tools — this is the beginning of stone tool technology. Its braincase measured 500–700 cubic centimetres.

The second major step in evolution was **Homo erectus** ("upright man"), dated to about 1.8 to 1.6 million years ago. This is a highly significant species for several reasons. Homo erectus had a strongly built skull with a much larger braincase of 800–1,200 cubic centimetres. This species manufactured the hand axe, which is considered the most distinctive stone tool of this period. More importantly, Homo erectus is believed to have discovered how to make and use **fire** — this discovery fundamentally changed human life by providing warmth, protection from wild animals, and the ability to cook food. Unlike Homo habilis, Homo erectus travelled vast distances, and its remains have been found not only in Africa but also in China, South Asia, and Southeast Asia.

The third step was the emergence of **Homo sapiens** ("wise man"), which appeared about 230,000 to 30,000 years ago. This species resembles the famous Neanderthal man found in western Germany. It had a short body and narrow forehead but a braincase of 1,200–1,800 cubic centimetres, representing a dramatic increase in brain capacity.

The final and most important stage of evolution was **Homo sapiens sapiens** — the fully modern human being. This species is traceable to about 115,000 years ago in southern Africa. It had a large rounded braincase of about 1,200–2,000 cubic centimetres. It was this species that developed complex and diverse stone tools for different functions and, later, symbolic thought, art, and language. It is believed that language as a faculty developed around 50,000 BC — an event of revolutionary significance for human social organization.

### Early Man in India

Only a few fossils relating to human evolution have been found in the Indian subcontinent. The earliest finds come from the **Siwalik hills** — specifically the Potwar plateau in Pakistani Punjab — where fossilized skulls called **Ramapithecus** and **Sivapithecus** have been found. These are considered roughly 2.2 million years old and appear to possess some hominid features, though they represent apes and not true humans.

The most significant discovery within India was made in 1982 in the **Narmada valley at Hathnora in Madhya Pradesh**, where an almost complete hominid skull was found. This skull was originally classified as Homo erectus but is now anatomically recognized as **archaic Homo sapiens**. This is known as **Narmada Man**.

From Sri Lanka, at a site called **Fa Hien cave**, fossils of Homo sapiens sapiens (modern humans) have been found, dated to about 34,000 years ago. This is considered the earliest Upper Palaeolithic site in the Indian subcontinent. Most importantly, modern genetic and archaeological evidence suggests that the earliest modern humans arrived in India from the south — through a coastal migration from Africa around 50,000 years ago — and not from the north.

## Unit 3: The Palaeolithic Age (Old Stone Age)

The Palaeolithic Age in India is divided into **three phases** — Lower, Middle, and Upper — classified on the basis of the type of stone tools used and the nature of climatic change. All three phases fall within the Pleistocene epoch (Ice Age).

### 3.1 Lower Palaeolithic Phase (600,000 – 150,000 BC)

The Lower Palaeolithic is the longest and earliest phase. In India, it is not older than 600,000 years — the site of **Bori in Maharashtra** is considered the earliest Lower Palaeolithic site in India and is dated to this period. The Early Old Stone Age in India may be associated with the Homo sapiens group.

The characteristic tools of this phase are **hand axes, cleavers, and choppers**. These are large, heavy, bifacially worked stone tools. The stone was worked on both sides to produce a sharp cutting edge. Stone tools were used for chopping, digging, and skinning animals. The people of this phase were principally **food gatherers** — they hunted small game, lived on fish and birds, and gathered plant food. They had no knowledge of agriculture or house construction. They lived in **caves and rock shelters**, which served as seasonal camps.

An important characteristic of this phase is that the axes found in India are broadly similar to those found in western Asia, Europe, and Africa, suggesting parallel cultural development. **Quartzite** was the most widely used raw material for making tools.

Important Lower Palaeolithic sites include:
- **Son/Sohan river valley** (now in Pakistani Punjab) — among the earliest sites
- **Belan valley** (Uttar Pradesh) — rich in stone tools; also yielded Middle and Upper Palaeolithic material at the same location
- **Didwana** (Rajasthan) — exceptionally important because it yielded Lower, Middle, and Upper Palaeolithic tools from the same location, providing a complete sequence
- **Chirki-Nevasa** (Maharashtra) — yielded as many as 2,000 tools
- **Nagarjunakonda** (Andhra Pradesh) — an important site
- **Bhimbetka rock shelters** (near Bhopal, Madhya Pradesh) — the rock shelters show features of the Lower Palaeolithic age and may have served as seasonal human camps

### 3.2 Middle Palaeolithic Phase (150,000 – 35,000 BC)

The Middle Palaeolithic industries in India were largely based upon **flakes** — small pieces of stone struck from a prepared core. This is a significant technological shift from the large bifacial tools of the Lower Palaeolithic. The principal tools of this phase are **blades, points, borers, and scrapers**, all made from flakes. These tools are lighter and smaller than their predecessors, indicating greater refinement and possibly more specialized uses.

The geographical distribution of Middle Palaeolithic sites broadly coincides with that of Lower Palaeolithic sites. Important locations include sites on the river **Narmada** and several places south of the **Tungabhadra river**. The **Belan valley** (Uttar Pradesh) is particularly notable as it lies at the foothills of the Vindhyas and is rich in both stone tools and animal fossils including cattle and deer — suggesting that these animals were hunted and possibly already in the early stages of domestication.

### 3.3 Upper Palaeolithic Phase (35,000 – 10,000 BC)

The Upper Palaeolithic is the final phase of the Old Stone Age. It coincides with the last phase of the Ice Age, when the climate became comparatively warmer. In the world context, this phase marks the appearance of new flint industries and the full dominance of modern humans (Homo sapiens sapiens).

As many as **566 Upper Palaeolithic sites** have been identified in India — a significantly larger number than the previous phases, possibly because the presence of grassland dotted with few trees made settlements more extensive. The characteristic tools of this phase are **blades and burins** (a burin is a chisel-like tool used for engraving). These have been found in Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Maharashtra, central Madhya Pradesh, southern Uttar Pradesh, Jharkhand, and adjoining areas.

Particularly important is the site of **Bhimbetka**, located 45 km south of Bhopal in the Vindhyan range. The rock shelters here contain paintings extending from the Upper Palaeolithic all the way through the Mesolithic age. Additionally, an Upper Palaeolithic assemblage — large flakes, blades, burins, and scrapers — has been found in the **upper levels of the Gujarat sand dunes**.

### 3.4 Rock Art of the Old Stone Age

The people of the Palaeolithic and Mesolithic ages were not merely tool-makers — they were also artists. **Bhimbetka in Madhya Pradesh** is the most remarkable prehistoric art site in India. Situated in the Vindhyan range, 45 km south of Bhopal, it has over 500 painted rock shelters distributed over an area of 10 square kilometres. The paintings at Bhimbetka extend from the Upper Palaeolithic to the Mesolithic age.

The subjects of the paintings are overwhelmingly animals — birds, animals, and human beings are depicted, but animals clearly dominate. This is not a coincidence. The people of this era depended almost entirely on hunting for survival, and scholars argue that they painted animals to symbolically assert control over them — a form of hunting magic. Only those animals that were hunted for food appear; grain-eating perching birds, for instance, do not figure in the earliest paintings. This strongly links the art to a hunting-gathering economy.

### 3.5 Social Organization in the Old Stone Age

The social organization of Palaeolithic people was extremely rudimentary. They appear to have lived in **bands** — small groups of about 25 persons formed for the purpose of hunting. Several bands could come together to form larger alliances of up to 500 persons. These alliances may have been ratified through collective rituals.

In the Upper Palaeolithic phase, members of a band shared the fruits of hunting and food gathering on an egalitarian basis — there was no concept of individual property. Formation of bands and alliances was likely facilitated by the emergence of **language**, which scholars now believe originated around 50,000 BC. The band eventually evolved into the **clan** during the Neolithic phase, where members were expected to marry outside their own group (exogamy).

## Unit 4: The Mesolithic Age (9,000 – 4,000 BC)

The Mesolithic or Middle Stone Age marked the **transition** between the Old Stone Age (Palaeolithic) and the New Stone Age (Neolithic). The Upper Palaeolithic age came to an end with the close of the Ice Age around 10,000 BC. The Pleistocene epoch gave way to the Holocene, and the climate became warm and rainy. These climatic changes brought about a transformation in fauna and flora — grasslands expanded, and populations of deer, antelope, sheep, goats, and cattle increased significantly. Humans took advantage of adequate rainfall, dense vegetation, and new forest resources.

In this new environment, around **9,000 BC**, there began an intermediate stage in Stone Age culture called the **Mesolithic age**. It is called intermediate because, while the first three occupations of Mesolithic people — hunting, fishing, and food gathering — continued the Palaeolithic tradition, the fourth occupation — the domestication of animals — was entirely new and pointed forward to the Neolithic culture. In this sense, the Mesolithic age was a transitional phase in the mode of subsistence, leading from pure hunting-gathering toward animal husbandry.

The Mesolithic culture in India lasted roughly from **9,000 BC to 4,000 BC**.

### 4.1 Characteristic Tools: The Microlithic Industry

The most distinctive feature of the Mesolithic age is the use of **microliths** — tiny, geometrically shaped stone tools. Unlike the large hand axes of the Palaeolithic, microliths are minute blades and geometric forms that were often hafted (attached) onto handles of wood or bone to create composite tools like saws, arrows, and sickles. The manufacturing of microliths required considerable skill and precision.

Mesolithic sites are found abundantly in **Rajasthan, southern Uttar Pradesh, central and eastern India**, and also south of the river Krishna.

### 4.2 Important Sites

**Bagor in Rajasthan** is the most extensively excavated Mesolithic site in India. It had a distinctive microlithic industry, and its inhabitants subsisted on hunting and pastoralism. The site was occupied continuously for as long as 5,000 years from the fifth millennium BC onwards, demonstrating remarkable cultural stability.

**Adamgarh in Madhya Pradesh** and **Bagor in Rajasthan** together provide the **earliest evidence for the domestication of animals** in the Indian part of the subcontinent — this is estimated to have occurred around **5,000 BC**. This is a crucial finding because it shows that the process of animal domestication was already beginning within the Mesolithic period, before the full Neolithic revolution.

From a study of deposits at **Sambhar** (formerly a salt lake in Rajasthan), there is evidence suggesting that the **cultivation of plants began around 7,000–6,000 BC** in Rajasthan. This, too, is a pre-Neolithic development of the greatest significance.

## Unit 5: The Neolithic Age (7,000 – 1,000 BC)

The Neolithic or New Stone Age was a revolutionary phase in human history. The defining feature of the Neolithic was the **combination of agriculture and animal domestication** that together created a food-producing economy. For the first time, humans were no longer entirely dependent on hunting, fishing, and gathering; they could now produce their own food. This change is so fundamental that historians call it the **Neolithic Revolution** — it laid the foundation for settled village life, surplus production, population growth, and ultimately civilization itself.

In the global context, the Neolithic began in western Asia around **9,000 BC**. In the Indian subcontinent, the earliest known Neolithic settlement dates to **7,000 BC** at **Mehrgarh** in Baluchistan. In India proper, some Neolithic sites to the north of the Vindhyas are as old as **5,000 BC**. Neolithic settlements in south India are generally not older than **2,500 BC**, and in some parts of southern and eastern India they are as late as **1,000 BC**. This shows that the Neolithic revolution reached different parts of India at very different times.

### 5.1 Technology: Polished Stone Tools

The people of the Neolithic age used tools and implements of **polished stone**. This distinguishes them sharply from Palaeolithic people who used unpolished, roughly worked stone. The most important Neolithic tool was the **stone axe**, which has been found in large numbers throughout the hilly tracts of India. The stone axe was put to various uses: clearing land, tilling soil, and constructing houses. Ancient legends connect this tool to the hero **Parashurama**, who is represented as an important axe-wielding figure — a cultural memory of the axe's centrality to this period.

Based on the types of axes used, scholars have identified three distinct regional groups of Neolithic settlements in India:
1. **North-western group** (Baluchistan and Kashmir valley) — distinguished by rectangular axes with a curved cutting edge
2. **North-eastern group** (Assam hills, Vindhyas, Bihar, UP) — distinguished by polished stone axes with a rectangular butt and occasional shouldered hoes
3. **Southern group** (Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, AP) — distinguished by axes with oval sides and a pointed butt

The Neolithic settlers also broke the ground with **stone hoes** and digging sticks weighted with ring stones, and used microlith blades. They lived in circular or rectangular houses made of mud and reed. Circular houses are associated with communal property; rectangular houses suggest individual ownership — a significant pointer to emerging social differentiation.

### 5.2 Mehrgarh — The Earliest Known Settlement

**Mehrgarh**, located on the bank of the Bolan river in the Kochi plain in Baluchistan (now Pakistan), is one of the most important Neolithic sites in the world. It is called the "bread basket" of Baluchistan and is considered one of the largest Neolithic settlements between the Indus and the Mediterranean.

The Neolithic people of Mehrgarh produced **wheat and barley** from the very beginning and domesticated cattle, sheep, and goats. Initially, goats predominated among domesticated animals, but eventually cattle outnumbered both goats and sheep. Cattle rearing likely supported agriculture by providing traction power. Cereals were stored in granaries made of mud bricks. The potter's wheel was known here from around 4,500 BC, and pots became painted during the period 4,500–3,500 BC. From the Kochi plain, agricultural settlements gradually expanded toward the Indus plain in this period. The 47 Later Neolithic settlements found in the dried basin of the Hakra river (a tributary of the Indus) eventually paved the way for the rise of the Harappan culture.

### 5.3 Kashmiri Neolithic — Burzahom and Gufkral

The Kashmiri Neolithic culture in the north-west had several distinctive features that set it apart from Neolithic cultures elsewhere in India. Its people lived in **pit dwellings** — houses dug into the earth, which provided insulation against the cold. They used both polished stone tools and a wide range of **bone tools** (weapons and implements made from bone) — a practice quite rare in Indian prehistory.

The most important site is **Burzahom**, which means "the place of birch." It is located 16 km north-west of Srinagar on the side of a lake. The people of Burzahom probably had an economy based on hunting and fishing, supplemented by agriculture and animal domestication. They used **coarse grey pottery**. The most remarkable and unique feature of the Burzahom culture is the practice of **burying domestic dogs in the graves of their masters** — a practice not found in any other Neolithic culture in India. The earliest date for Burzahom is approximately **2,700 BC**.

**Gufkral** (which literally means "cave of the potter"), located 41 km south-west of Srinagar, is another important Kashmiri Neolithic site. Its people practised both agriculture and animal husbandry.

### 5.4 Chirand — The Bone-Tool Site of Bihar

The only other site in India (besides the Kashmir valley) that has yielded considerable bone implements is **Chirand**, located 40 km west of Patna on the northern side of the Ganges. The bone implements found here are made of **antler** (the horns of deer), suggesting that deer were hunted extensively in the forested area nearby.

Chirand is situated at the junction of four rivers — the Ganges, Son, Gandak, and Ghaghra — which made it an ideal location for settlement. The bones recovered from Chirand cannot be dated earlier than **2,000 BC**, placing it in the late Neolithic phase. The site is notable for a paucity of stone tools (stone being unavailable in the riverine plain), which is why bone was used extensively as a substitute material.

### 5.5 North-Eastern Neolithic Settlements

Neolithic tools have also been recovered from the hills of **Assam** and the **Garo hills in Meghalaya**. On the northern spurs of the Vindhyas, particularly in **Mirzapur and Allahabad districts of Uttar Pradesh**, several Neolithic settlements have been found. The sites of **Koldihwa and Mahagra** in Allahabad district are especially significant because they provide evidence for **rice cultivation as early as the fifth millennium BC** — placing among the earliest rice-farming evidence in the world. **Senuwar** in the Rohtas district in the Kaimur hills is considered the most important site in this sub-region.

### 5.6 Neolithic Settlements in South India

South India has the **largest number of Neolithic settlements** in the entire country — over **850 settlements** spread across Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, and Tamil Nadu. This density of settlements is explained by the easy availability of stone in the region, which was essential for making Neolithic tools.

The South Indian Neolithic people typically settled on the **tops of granite hills** or on plateaus near river banks. They used stone axes, stone blades, and stone querns (grinding stones) for grinding corn, demonstrating that they were engaged in cereal cultivation. Fire-baked earthen figurines found at these sites depict cattle, sheep, and goats in large numbers, confirming that animal husbandry was an important part of their economy.

Important excavated sites include **Maski, Brahmagiri, Hallur, Kodekal, Sanganakallu, Piklihal, and Takkalakota** in Karnataka, **Paiyampalli** in Tamil Nadu, and **Utnur** in AP. The Neolithic phase in south India broadly covered the period **2,400 BC to 1,000 BC**.

The site of **Piklihal** provides a remarkable window into the pastoral character of south Indian Neolithic life. The settlers here were cattle-herders who domesticated cattle, sheep, and goats. They set up seasonal camps surrounded by cowpens — enclosures made with posts and stakes where dung accumulated. When it was time to move on, the entire camping ground was set on fire and cleared, leaving behind the characteristic **ash mounds** that archaeologists use to identify this culture. Both ash mounds and permanent habitation sites have been found at Piklihal.

The main crops cultivated in south India were **ragi** (finger millet) and **horse gram** (kulathi), along with some rice. This is different from the north-western Neolithic (Mehrgarh), where wheat and barley were the main crops — indicating distinct regional agricultural traditions.

### 5.7 Pottery and Other Developments

The Neolithic age also saw the beginning of **pottery**. As agricultural communities began to produce and store grain, they needed vessels for storage, cooking, eating, and drinking. Pottery therefore first appeared in this phase. Early Neolithic pottery was handmade. Later, Neolithic people used foot-powered wheels to make pots — the potter's wheel is believed to have come to Baluchistan from western Asia and spread from there across the subcontinent. Neolithic pottery types include **black-burnished ware, grey ware, and mat-impressed ware**.

### 5.8 Progress and Limitations of the Neolithic Age

The Neolithic age represented a revolutionary change in the mode of human subsistence. People were no longer wholly dependent on hunting and gathering; cultivation and cattle husbandry now provided them with food. With their tools, they also built permanent dwellings. These developments placed humanity on the threshold of civilization.

However, the Neolithic people faced a fundamental limitation — their entire technology was based on stone. This meant they could not establish large settlements in the vast open plains far from hills, because stone was not available there. They could settle only on the slopes of hills, in rock shelters, or in hilly river valleys. Even with great effort, they were unable to produce more than they needed for bare subsistence. This limitation was not overcome until the Iron Age made it possible to clear the hard soil of the Gangetic plains on a large scale.

## Unit 6: The Chalcolithic Age (Copper-Stone Phase) (c. 3,000 – 700 BC)

The Chalcolithic age marks the transition from the Neolithic to the Metal Age. The word "Chalcolithic" is derived from the Greek words *chalcos* (copper) and *lithos* (stone) — meaning the **Copper-Stone phase**, when copper was used alongside stone implements. It was the first metal to be used by human beings.

The Chalcolithic stage is technically applied to the **pre-Harappan phase** in the Indian context. However, in many parts of India, Chalcolithic cultures actually followed the Bronze Age Harappan civilization and existed independently of it. What distinguishes Chalcolithic cultures from the Bronze Age Harappan culture is that while the Harappans had urbanized on the basis of bronze technology and flood-plain agriculture, Chalcolithic people remained essentially **rural communities** spread over a wide area.

Chalcolithic people mostly used stone and copper objects. They also occasionally used low-grade bronze and even iron in small amounts. They were primarily rural communities settled in semi-arid areas with hilly terrain and rivers. Chalcolithic settlements in India are concentrated in **south-eastern Rajasthan, western Madhya Pradesh, western Maharashtra, and eastern and southern India**.

### 6.1 Major Chalcolithic Cultures and Sites

**Ahar (Tambavati) and Gilund — South-eastern Rajasthan**

In the dry zones of the Banas valley in south-eastern Rajasthan, two important Chalcolithic sites were excavated: **Ahar** and **Gilund**. Ahar's original name was **Tambavati**, meaning "the place with copper" — an indication of its close association with copper metallurgy. The people of Ahar practised copper smelting from the very outset. Unlike other contemporary Chalcolithic farming cultures, Ahar used virtually no microlithic tools; instead, its copper objects were numerous — flat axes, bangles, and copper sheets have all been found here. The Ahar culture is dated to approximately **2,100–1,500 BC**. Gilund, which serves as a regional centre, shows only fragments of copper but had a stone blade industry. Flat rectangular copper axes were found at Jorwe and Chandoli in Maharashtra, and copper chisels at Chandoli.

**Kayatha and Eran — Western Madhya Pradesh (Malwa)**

In western Madhya Pradesh, the Chalcolithic sites of **Kayatha and Eran** have been excavated. The **Malwa-ware** — the pottery characteristic of the Malwa Chalcolithic culture of central and western India — is considered the richest among Chalcolithic ceramics. Some Malwa pottery and related cultural elements also appear in Maharashtra.

**The Jorwe Culture — Western Maharashtra**

The most extensively excavated Chalcolithic region is **western Maharashtra**, where the **Jorwe culture** flourished. This culture takes its name from the type-site of **Jorwe village**, located on the left bank of the Pravara river (a tributary of the Godavari) in Ahmadnagar district. The Jorwe culture covered the period **c. 1400 to 700 BC** and encompassed all of modern Maharashtra except parts of Vidarbha and the Konkan coastal region.

Although the Jorwe culture was fundamentally rural, some of its settlements — notably **Daimabad** and **Inamgaon** — had almost reached an urban scale. All these Maharashtra sites were located in semi-arid areas on **brown-black soil** with *ber* and *babul* vegetation, situated in riverine tracts. In addition, **Navdatoli** on the Narmada river was also part of this broader Chalcolithic zone.

Of the 200 Jorwe sites discovered so far, the largest is **Daimabad** in the Godavari valley. It extends over about 20 hectares and could have accommodated approximately 4,000 people. It also seems to have been fortified with a mud wall that had stone rubble bastions. Daimabad is famous for the recovery of many **bronze goods**, some of which show influence of the Harappan culture.

**Inamgaon** (Pune district) was another major Chalcolithic settlement. In the earlier Chalcolithic phase, large mud houses with ovens and circular pit houses have been discovered there. In the later phase (1300–1000 BC), a large house with five rooms — four rectangular and one circular — was found at the centre of the settlement. This central house is believed to have been the residence of the **chief**. A granary located close to it may have been used for storing tribute collected in kind. Inamgaon had over a hundred houses and numerous grave sites. The settlement was fortified and surrounded by a moat, indicating the need for defence.

**Navdatoli — Narmada Valley**

**Navdatoli** on the bank of the Narmada river in Madhya Pradesh is remarkable for one very specific reason: archaeologists have found here the widest variety of food crops known from any single prehistoric site in India. The cereals found include **wheat, rice, lentil (masur), black gram, green gram, grass pea, ber, linseed, cotton, bajra, rai,** and several millets. This extraordinary diversity of cultivation demonstrates how advanced Chalcolithic agriculture had become.

**Ganeshwar — Rajasthan (Copper Belt Site)**

**Ganeshwar** is located near the rich copper mines of the Sikar-Jhunjhunu area in the **Khetri copper belt of Rajasthan**. The copper objects found here are remarkable in their variety: arrowheads, spearheads, fish hooks, bangles, and chisels. The Ganeshwar culture is dated to **2,800–2,200 BC**, which means it predates the mature Harappan culture. It is believed that Ganeshwar principally **supplied copper objects to Harappa** and received little in return. The Ganeshwar assemblage includes microliths and other stone tools alongside copper, and it also shows the **Ochre-Coloured Pottery (OCP)** — a red-slipped ware painted in black, largely in vase forms. Ganeshwar is best understood as a **pre-Harappan Chalcolithic culture** that contributed to the making of the mature Harappan civilization.

**Eastern India — Pandu Rajar Dhibi and Other Sites**

In eastern India, important Chalcolithic sites include **Pandu Rajar Dhibi** in Burdwan district and **Mahishdal** in Birbhum district in West Bengal; and **Senuar, Sonpur, and Taradih** in Bihar. These mid-Gangetic and lower-Gangetic sites relate to approximately 1,500–700 BC. The mid-Gangetic Chalcolithic settlements show little use of copper and relied more heavily on stone tools.

### 6.2 Agriculture and Economy

The Chalcolithic people were settled agricultural communities. They reared **cows, sheep, goats, pigs, and buffaloes** and also hunted deer. Remains of the camel have also been found, but they were generally not acquainted with the horse. The people certainly ate beef and pork, though pork consumption was not on a considerable scale.

Their agriculture was diverse and productive. Beyond the cereals already mentioned in connection with Navdatoli, it is important to note that **cotton** was cultivated in the black cotton soil of the Deccan. In the lower Deccan, **ragi, bajra, and several millets** were cultivated. In eastern India — specifically Bihar and West Bengal — **fish hooks** have been found, suggesting that the Chalcolithic people there lived on a diet of fish and rice, a tradition that continues in eastern India to the present day.

### 6.3 Pottery — The Black-and-Red Ware

One of the most distinctive markers of Chalcolithic culture is the **black-and-red ware** pottery. This pottery was thrown on the wheel — meaning it was made using a rotating potter's wheel — and was occasionally painted with **white linear designs**. It became widely prevalent from nearly 2,000 BC onwards and has been found across a vast geographical area encompassing Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Bihar, and West Bengal.

However, an important caution is necessary here: it would be **wrong to assume** that all people who used black-and-red pottery belonged to the same culture. Regional differences were significant. In Maharashtra, MP, and Bihar, people produced channel-spouted pots, dishes-on-stand, and bowls-on-stand. In eastern India, painted examples of this pottery are very rare. The pottery is a cultural marker, not proof of cultural uniformity.

### 6.4 Architecture

The Chalcolithic people were generally **not acquainted with burnt bricks**. Burnt bricks were rarely used; when they were, it was a late development (as at Gilund around 1,500 BC). Most Chalcolithic houses were built of **mud brick, wattle and daub** (a framework of wooden poles and reeds plastered with mud), and **thatched roofs**. The people of Ahar, however, lived in stone houses.

Houses were either circular or rectangular. In circular or round huts, property was likely held in common; rectangular houses indicate emerging individual household ownership. The settlement pattern at Inamgaon — where craftsmen lived on the western fringes while the chief occupied the large house at the centre — suggests a beginning of **social stratification**.

### 6.5 Arts, Crafts, and Trade

The Chalcolithic people were clearly skilled artisans. They were expert **coppersmiths** and skilful workers in stone. Tools, weapons, and bangles of copper have been unearthed at multiple sites. They manufactured beads of semiprecious stones — **carnelian, steatite, and quartz crystal** — indicating both an aesthetic sensibility and access to trade networks that brought these materials from distant sources.

Spinning and weaving were known, as evidenced by spindle whorls found in Malwa. Cotton flax and silk threads made of cotton silk and *semal* silk (from the cotton tree) have been found in Maharashtra — demonstrating an expertise in textile manufacture. At Inamgaon, there is evidence of occupational specialization: the site had **potters, smiths, ivory carvers, lime makers, and terracotta artisans** living within the same settlement.

### 6.6 Religious Beliefs and Burial Practices

The religious and ritual life of Chalcolithic communities can be partly reconstructed from archaeological evidence. **Terracotta figurines of women** found at multiple sites suggest that Chalcolithic people **venerated the mother goddess** — a widespread belief in early agricultural societies. A figure of the mother goddess similar to those found in western Asia has been found at Inamgaon. In Malwa and Rajasthan, stylized **bull terracottas** suggest that the bull was a symbol of a religious cult.

Chalcolithic burial practices are informative about both beliefs and social structure. In Maharashtra, people **buried their dead beneath the floors of their own houses**, positioning the body in a **north-to-south direction**. They did not use separate cemeteries. Pots and copper objects were deposited in the graves — clearly for the use of the dead in the next world, indicating a belief in an afterlife.

### 6.7 Social Inequalities

The Chalcolithic phase is significant for the **first visible signs of social inequality** in peninsular India. A settlement hierarchy is visible across Jorwe settlements in Maharashtra — some settlements are as large as twenty hectares, while others cover only five hectares or less. The larger settlements likely dominated the smaller ones.

Within settlements, the chief and his kin lived in **rectangular houses**, while the common people lived in **round huts**. At Inamgaon, craftsmen lived on the western fringes of the settlement and the chief at the centre. In the graves at **Chandoli and Nevasa** (western Maharashtra), some children were buried with **copper-based necklaces**, while others had graves with only plain pottery — a clear indicator of wealth differences even in childhood. At Kayatha, twenty-nine copper bangles and two unique axes were found in a single house, along with necklaces of semiprecious stones — unmistakably the possessions of an affluent household.

### 6.8 The Copper Hoards and Ochre-Coloured Pottery (OCP) Culture

One of the most intriguing aspects of the Chalcolithic period is the discovery of **copper hoards** — large collections of copper objects buried together. Over **eighty copper hoards** have been found across a vast area stretching from West Bengal and Odisha in the east to Gujarat and Haryana in the west, and from Andhra Pradesh in the south to UP in the north. These hoards contain a wide variety of objects: rings, celts (axe-like tools), hatchets, swords, harpoons, spearheads, and **anthropomorphic figures** (human-shaped objects). The largest hoard comes from **Gungeria in Madhya Pradesh**, comprising 424 copper tools and weapons along with 102 thin sheets of silver objects.

A substantial number of these copper hoards are concentrated in the **Ganga-Yamuna doab**. In excavations at two places in western UP, some of these copper objects have been found in association with **ochre-coloured pots** (OCP) and some mud structures, giving rise to the name **Ochre-Coloured Pottery culture**. This culture is dated roughly between **2,000 and 1,500 BC**. The OCP people used a red-slipped ware often painted in black, largely in vase forms. The settlement of **Jodhpura** on the Haryana-Rajasthan border shows the thickest OCP deposits of 1.1 metres.

### 6.9 Limitations and Decline of Chalcolithic Cultures

Despite their achievements, Chalcolithic cultures had significant limitations that ultimately prevented them from developing into urban civilizations on their own.

First, the Chalcolithic people **did not milk their cattle** — they slaughtered domesticated animals for food rather than using them for dairy products. This tribal practice, similar to that of the Gonds of Bastar even today, meant they could not make full use of their animals as an economic resource. Second, **no hoe or plough** has been found at Chalcolithic sites. They used perforated stone discs tied as weights to digging sticks, which was suitable only for slash-and-burn (*jhum*) cultivation on light soils. Intensive cultivation of the **black cotton soil** of central and western India required iron implements — and iron was absent from Chalcolithic culture. This is why Chalcolithic communities could not permanently settle on the fertile alluvial plains.

Third, the **supply of copper was limited**, and Chalcolithic people did not know how to mix tin with copper to create the much stronger metal **bronze**. Bronze technology was what enabled the rise of the earliest urban civilizations in Crete, Egypt, Mesopotamia, and the Indus Valley.

Finally, Chalcolithic people **could not write** and **did not live in cities**. These elements of civilization first appeared in India only with the Harappan (Indus) civilization.

The result of these limitations was that Chalcolithic cultures in central and western India disappeared by **1,200 BC**. Only the **Jorwe culture** in Maharashtra continued until **700 BC**. In western India and western MP, the decline is attributed partly to a **fall in rainfall from about 1,200 BC onwards**. In the red soil areas of eastern India, however, the Chalcolithic phase was immediately followed by the iron phase, which transformed the people into full-fledged agriculturalists without any gap.

### 6.10 Importance of the Chalcolithic Phase

Despite its limitations, the Chalcolithic phase holds a very important place in Indian history. It was during this phase that:
- The **first large villages** in peninsular India were established
- Far **more varieties of cereals** were cultivated than were known to Neolithic communities
- **Painted pottery** was used for the first time in India (they were the first pre-Bronze Age culture to paint their pots)
- **Social inequalities** and a settlement hierarchy first became visible
- **Pre-Harappan Chalcolithic farming communities** (such as those at Amri, Kot Diji, Kalibangan, Ganeshwar) created the foundations and conditions for the rise of the mature Harappan urban civilization

## Summary: The Broad Arc of Change

The story of prehistoric India is a story of slow but continuous human progress across hundreds of thousands of years. The Palaeolithic hunter-gatherer, entirely dependent on what nature provided and constantly moving in search of food, gave way gradually to the Mesolithic herder, who had begun to domesticate animals and tentatively cultivate plants. The Neolithic revolution then fundamentally transformed the human condition by establishing settled agricultural villages and creating a food surplus. The Chalcolithic age added the use of copper and significantly expanded agricultural production and village complexity, including the beginnings of social stratification. Each phase built upon the previous one, and together they created the human, agricultural, and technological foundations upon which India's first great civilization — the Harappan — was eventually built.
